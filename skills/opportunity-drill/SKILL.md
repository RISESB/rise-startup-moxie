# Opportunity Drill

A disciplined pass that turns a raw hunch into something specific enough to be proven wrong. The core loop: notice a signal, check it against evidence, stress test it, find the gap, build a test.

Rules of the drill:
- **Evidence before opinions. Costs before revenue.**
- If the drill kills the idea, that's a win. Move to the next one.
- Name real groups of people ("the front-desk person at a 2-dentist office"), never "small businesses" or "everyone."
- Never invent a number, a company, a price or a quote. If something is unknown, say it's unknown.
- End with one test, not five.

## What you need from the student
- The signal: what they noticed, in their own words.
- What they already know: who they've talked to, what they've seen, what they've tried.

## Step 1: Frame it
Work these out with the student, one at a time:
- **Hypothesis:** "[specific kind of person] spends [time or money] on [problem] because [root cause], and would pay for [solution]."
- **Pain moment:** the specific recurring moment the pain shows up. A day, a task, a trigger.
- **Why now:** what changed recently that makes this worth doing today. If nothing changed, say so.
- **Kill criteria:** 2 to 4 specific, checkable things that would each kill the idea.

## Step 2: Who hurts, and how we know
List 2 to 4 groups of people who feel this problem. For each: what it's like in their day, **how we know** (the student saw it, someone told them, or "we are guessing"), and how bad it is (a little, a lot, don't know).

Guesses are fine. Hiding that they're guesses is not.

## Step 3: Map the value chain
Every business sits in a chain of people who hand work to each other. For a dental office: patients, front desk, dentist, insurance company, software vendor, supplier. The first idea is usually aimed at the most obvious link. Often a neighboring link has the same pain, more money, or less competition.

Map 3 to 5 links for this signal. For each: what they do, what's hard for them, what someone could sell them, and whether it's worth a look (strong, maybe, weak). Then say honestly which link looks best and why.

## Step 4: Stress test
Give each a green, amber or red, with one sentence tied to something real:
1. **Pain is real:** do we have evidence, or a hunch?
2. **Reachable:** can the student actually find and talk to these customers?
3. **Market size:** enough of them nearby to matter?
4. **Competition beatable:** who already does this, and where are they weak? (If unknown, that's amber and a question to answer.)
5. **The math:** could one sale cover its costs?
6. **Testable this week:** with what the student has now?
7. **Your edge:** what does this student have that makes them the right one to try? (Bird-in-Hand: who you are, what you know, who you know.)

## Step 5: Score and decide
Give a score from 0 to 100 and a blunt one-sentence verdict: worth a week of work or not, and why. Say what would most likely kill it.

## Step 6: One test
Design the smallest test that could prove the riskiest assumption wrong this week:
- "If I [action], then [measurable result], which proves [what]."
- Up to 5 steps, by day, each with a quantity.
- The exact number that means keep going, and the exact number that means stop.
- What it costs in hours and dollars.
- What they learn even if it fails.

## Output
1. **Headline:** one plain sentence on what's going on here.
2. **Hypothesis, pain moment, why now, kill criteria**
3. **Who hurts** (table: group, what it's like, how we know, how bad)
4. **Value chain** (table: link, what they do, their pain, what you could sell, worth a look)
5. **Best link:** which one, and the one-sentence reason.
6. **Stress test** (the 7 lights)
7. **Score and verdict**
8. **Dig here:** up to 4 unanswered questions, each with a way the student could answer it this week (who to ask, what to count).
9. **This week's test**
10. **Watch out:** the single biggest reason this might not work.

## Getting real evidence
You can't search the web here, so everything in this drill comes from what the student knows. To check it against the outside world (who else sells this, what they charge, how many customers exist nearby), offer to file a research request with `request_help`. The overnight run can look it up and email back a table with sources. Put the hypothesis, the customer, the place, and the 3 or 4 questions from "Dig here" in the brief.
