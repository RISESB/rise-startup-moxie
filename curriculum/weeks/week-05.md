# Week 5: AI Basics and Outreach

Unit 1: Foundations & Identity

**Guiding question:** How do entrepreneurs use AI as a thinking partner and outreach tool — not a shortcut?

_When it last ran: Week of September 22, 2025 · 7:30–9:00 AM · Mon–Fri. Dates are from a previous year; this year's calendar may differ._

## By the end of the week, students can
- Explain the difference between using AI as a crutch vs. a tool.
- Use Claude and ChatGPT to research, draft outreach, and stress-test ideas.
- Write and send at least one real outreach message using AI assistance.
- Evaluate AI output critically — identifying what is useful and what needs human judgment.

## Lesson walkthrough
_Written as the facilitator's script for the classroom. Use it to know what was taught and how; don't read it back to students._

### Monday

### Monday — Lecture: AI Basics — How Entrepreneurs Use AI

Facilitator Goal: Students understand AI as a business tool — not a homework shortcut. They leave knowing the rules, the capabilities, and the limits of Claude and ChatGPT for entrepreneurial work.

#### 7:30–7:35 AM — Warm-Up

"Who has used ChatGPT or Claude before? For what?" Show of hands, then 3-4 students share.

Listen for: homework, writing essays, looking things up. Then say: "That is one use case. Today we talk about a very different one."

#### 7:35–8:05 AM — Lecture: AI Basics

30 minutes.

Opening (2 min)

Say: "AI is not going to replace entrepreneurs. But entrepreneurs who know how to use AI will outcompete those who don't."

"The question is not whether to use it. The question is how to use it well."

Talking Point 1 — What AI Is and Is Not (5 min)

Say: "AI tools like Claude and ChatGPT are prediction engines. They predict the most likely next word based on patterns in enormous amounts of text."

"That makes them incredibly useful for certain things. And completely unreliable for others."

AI is good at:

Drafting — emails, pitches, descriptions, outlines

Research summaries — explaining concepts, comparing options

Brainstorming — generating options you might not have considered

Stress-testing — poking holes in your plan, identifying weak spots

AI is NOT good at:

Current real-time information — it has a knowledge cutoff

Knowing your specific market or customers

Making decisions for you — it will sound confident even when it is wrong

Replacing the real conversations you need to have with actual customers

Say: "AI is a bionic arm — it extends what you can do. It is not a robot that replaces you. The thinking still has to be yours."

Talking Point 2 — The 3 Rules (5 min)

Write on the board:

Rule 1: AI is a bionic arm, not a robot. You do the thinking — AI helps you execute faster.

Rule 2: Focus on problems. Use AI to go deeper on real problems, not to shortcut the work of understanding them.

Rule 3: Stay safe. Do not put sensitive information — personal data, your business's unreleased plans, anything you would not want public — into a free AI tool. Use a business account when possible.

Say: "These are not school rules. These are professional rules that real entrepreneurs follow."

Talking Point 3 — Claude vs. ChatGPT (5 min)

Say: "We use two tools this year: Claude (made by Anthropic) and ChatGPT (made by OpenAI). They are similar but have different strengths."

ChatGPT: Strong at brainstorming, structured outputs, and general tasks. Widely used — more tutorials and resources available.

Claude: Strong at nuanced writing, longer documents, and following complex instructions. Often better at sounding like a human.

"You will get good at both. For most tasks this year, either one works. When in doubt, try the same prompt in both and compare the outputs."

Talking Point 4 — Prompting Well (8 min)

Say: "The output you get is only as good as the prompt you give. Garbage in, garbage out."

Three elements of a strong prompt:

Context — tell the AI who you are and what situation you are in

Task — be specific about what you want it to do

Format — tell it how you want the output

Bad prompt: "Write me an email to a potential customer."

Good prompt: "I am a 17-year-old starting a lawn care business in South Bend, Indiana. I want to reach out to homeowners in my neighborhood who might need weekly lawn maintenance. Write me a short, friendly cold outreach email — no more than 5 sentences — that sounds like a real person, not a corporation."

Demo this live: type the bad prompt, show the output. Then type the good prompt, show the difference.

Ask: "What made the second prompt better?" Let students respond.

Closing (3 min)

Say: "AI levels the playing field. A 17-year-old with a laptop and a good prompt can research, write, and outreach at a level that used to require a full team."

"Tomorrow you are going to use it for real — to research your market and draft your first outreach message."

#### 8:05–8:50 AM — Live AI Practice

Students open Claude or ChatGPT on their laptops.

Task 1: "Prompt the AI to explain your business idea back to you as if it were a customer — not a business person."

Task 2: "Prompt the AI to find 3 weaknesses in your May-B-P concept."

Task 3: "Prompt the AI to suggest 3 potential first customers for your business — be as specific as possible in your prompt."

After each task: "Does the output surprise you? What did it get right? What did it miss?"

Walk the room. Help students improve their prompts when the output is generic.

#### 8:50–9:00 AM — Close

Preview Tuesday: "Tomorrow you write a real outreach message and send it — or prepare to send it — to an actual potential customer."

### Tuesday

### Tuesday — Activities: AI as a Business Tool + Outreach Workshop

Facilitator Goal: Students use AI to research their market and draft a real outreach message to a potential customer.

#### 7:30–7:35 AM — Warm-Up

"What was the most useful thing AI told you yesterday? What was the most wrong?" 3-4 students share.

#### 7:35–8:00 AM — Market Research with AI

Students use Claude or ChatGPT to research their target customer and market.

Prompt guide — write on the board:

"Who are the most likely customers for [your business]? Describe them in detail — demographics, habits, pain points."

"What do people who have [the problem you're solving] currently do about it? What are the existing solutions and what are their weaknesses?"

"What questions should I be asking potential customers about [the problem]?"

Students write down 3 insights from their research. These feed into their outreach message.

#### 8:00–8:45 AM — Outreach Workshop

Say: "You are going to write a real outreach message to one potential customer. This could be an email, a DM, or a text — whatever makes sense for who you are trying to reach."

"Use AI to help you draft it. Then edit it until it sounds like you — not a robot."

Prompt template to get them started:

"I am [age], starting a [type of business] in [location]. My potential customer is [describe]. Write me a short outreach message — [email/DM/text] — that introduces me, explains the problem I solve, and asks for a 10-minute conversation. Keep it under [X] words. Make it sound like a real person."

After drafting: Pairs read each other's messages out loud. Ask: "Does this sound like the person sitting next to you? Or does it sound like a press release?"

Revise until it sounds human.

Goal: Every student leaves with a message they are willing to actually send.

#### 8:45–9:00 AM — Speaker Tour Prep

Distribute Speaker Tour Prep Worksheets. Students prepare for Wednesday.

### Wednesday

### Wednesday — Speaker/Tour

Run the speaker or tour per standard Wednesday format. Prep sheets out, active listening, notes captured.

### Thursday

### Thursday — Speaker Recap + Thank You Cards + AI Outreach Work Day

#### 7:30–7:35 AM — Warm-Up

"One sentence — most interesting thing from Wednesday." Around the room, no repeats.

#### 7:35–7:55 AM — Speaker Recap

Two columns: "What we heard" | "How this applies to our outreach or research"

Ask: "Did the speaker say anything that would change how you reach out to customers?"

#### 7:55–8:20 AM — Thank You Cards

Handwritten. Specific. Required elements:

One specific thing you heard or observed

One question it sparked

A genuine, specific thank you

Walk the room. Hand back generic cards.

#### 8:20–9:00 AM — AI Outreach Work Day

Students finalize their outreach message and send it — or schedule it to send.

If a student has already sent it: "Follow up with AI. Prompt: 'I sent this outreach message [paste it]. If I don't hear back in 3 days, write me a short follow-up message.'"

Use this time for 1-on-1s. Key question: "Who did you reach out to? What do you expect them to say?"

### Friday

### Friday — Deal or No Deal + 1-on-1s + Week Reflection

Facilitator Goal: Build negotiation thinking. Tie to AI — some deals this week involve AI tools or decisions.

#### 7:30–7:35 AM — Warm-Up

"Tell the person next to you: what is the most useful thing you used AI for this week?" 90 seconds. 2-3 share.

#### 7:35–8:05 AM — Deal or No Deal (Week 5 — Rotation: Deal or No Deal)

Same format. One student as entrepreneur, facilitator as deal-maker, class votes.

Week 5 deals:

Deal 1: "An AI tool will automate your entire customer outreach for $99/month. It will send 50 messages a day on your behalf. Deal or no deal?"

Follow up: "What are the risks of fully automating outreach? What might you lose?"

Deal 2: "A company wants to license your business idea for $5,000. You get the money but can never build the business yourself. Deal or no deal?"

Follow up: "How do you decide if an idea is worth $5,000 vs. worth building yourself?"

Deal 3: "A local business will give you free office space if you manage their social media for 5 hours a week. Deal or no deal?"

Follow up: "How do you calculate whether a trade like this is worth it?"

#### 8:05–8:40 AM — 1-on-1 Sessions

Students work independently: refine outreach, continue May-B-P, or journal.

1-on-1 focus:

"Did you send your outreach? What happened?"

"How are you using AI in your business work — and where are you still doing it all yourself?"

#### 8:40–8:55 AM — Week Reflection

Ask:

"What surprised you about using AI as a business tool this week?"

"Where did AI help? Where did it fall short?"

"What is one thing you will use AI for every week going forward?"

#### 8:55–9:00 AM — Preview Week 6

Week 6: Practice Your Pitch — Micro Pitch #1 + Networking Skills.

"Next week you pitch your business idea for the first time. Start thinking now: what is your one-sentence pitch? Who is your customer, what problem do you solve, and why should anyone care?"
