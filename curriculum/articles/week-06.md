# Practice Your Pitch

_Most students think a pitch is a mini-presentation you get through. This week we teach them it is a conversation starter, and we make them run it in front of a real room._

Ask a student to pitch their business and watch what happens. They start with "So basically what I do is," they talk for two minutes, and then they stop, having explained everything and asked for nothing. The person across from them nods politely and the moment is gone.

That is the default, and it is the thing Week 6 exists to fix.

A pitch is not a presentation. A pitch is a conversation starter. The goal is not to explain everything about your business. The goal is to make the other person want to know more. Most first pitches are long, vague, and forgettable, and this is the week we take that apart.

## Problem, Solution, Ask

We put three words on the board and build the whole week on them:

> Problem → Solution → Ask.

Each one has a job.

- **Problem** - start with the pain, not the product. What problem exists, who has it, and how does it affect them?
- **Solution** - what you do about it, in one sentence. Keep it simple.
- **Ask** - what you actually want from this conversation. A customer, a connection, a piece of advice. Be specific.

The example we walk through in class is a senior rideshare service. Problem: a lot of seniors in South Bend can't drive and rideshares feel unsafe to them. Solution: a local rideshare built for seniors, with vetted drivers and familiar faces. Ask: two or three seniors to pilot it for free in the next 30 days, do you know anyone?

Then we ask the room what made it work. They usually get there on their own. Specific problem, specific solution, specific ask, no jargon. It sounds like a real person, not a student reading a report.

## What kills a pitch

Naming the failure modes out loud does more than any amount of praise. So we list the pitch killers and tell students we are going to practice until none of them show up.

- **Too long** - if you need more than 60 seconds, you don't know your idea well enough yet.
- **No ask** - you described your business and then waited. For what?
- **Vague problem** - "I help people be more productive" tells me nothing.
- **Reading from notes** - if you can't say it without the paper, you're not ready.

None of these are hard to fix. They just have to be seen first, which is why we say them plainly instead of letting students discover them the hard way on Thursday.

## Networking is a skill, not a personality

The other half of the week is the part students dread, so we reframe it the same way. Networking is not standing in a corner waiting for someone to talk to you. It is a skill with a shape, and we teach a three-step version.

- **Introduce with intention** - "Hi, I'm [name]. I'm working on [one sentence about your business]. What do you do?" Never "Hi, I'm a student." That ends the conversation before it starts.
- **Ask good questions** - "How did you get started?" "What's the hardest part of your business right now?" "Who else should I be talking to?" Skip the generic ones like "any advice for young entrepreneurs?"
- **Follow up within 24 hours** - a short message that references something specific you talked about. Most people never do this, which is exactly why it works.

## How the week actually runs

The framework is Monday. The rest of the week is reps.

Tuesday is a workshop. Students say their pitch three or four times to different partners, rotating every few minutes, revising between rounds. Feedback is boxed into two sentence starters so it stays useful: "the clearest part was" and "I was confused by." We also let them run a draft through Claude or ChatGPT with a narrow prompt - "what is the weakest sentence in this pitch, and how would you make it stronger?" - because a sharp question to AI beats a vague one. Then we simulate a networking event so Thursday isn't the first time.

Thursday is Micro Pitch #1. Every student comes to the front, 60 seconds, timer running. The class writes one thing that was clear and one question the pitch raised on a sticky note. The facilitator gives one specific piece of feedback, never "great job," but something like "your problem statement was strong, your ask was unclear."

## Where students get stuck

Three things go wrong, and all three have a move.

They run long. When they hit 60 seconds, stop them gently: "You're at 60. Land the plane." The constraint is the lesson.

They freeze. Point them back to the framework: "Start with the problem. Who has it?" The structure is there so they always have a next sentence.

They drop the ask. This is the most common one. They describe the business beautifully and then trail off. The fix is always the same - end with something specific you want.

And underneath all of it, the nerves. We tell them nervous is fine. Nervous means you care. Standing up and saying it out loud matters more than whether it came out clean.

## What good looks like by Friday

By Friday you are not looking for a polished founder. You are looking for a student who can deliver a 60-second pitch with a real problem, a one-sentence solution, and a specific ask, and who can walk up to a stranger and start a conversation without leading with "I'm just a student."

Friday we run Profit or Broke using businesses students actually pitched, so the numbers feel personal. Then 1-on-1s, where the question that matters is the one that stings a little: what feedback landed, and what are you fixing before next time?

This is also the end of Unit 1, so we stop and mark it. Six weeks ago most of these students thought a pitch was a speech and entrepreneurship was for other people. Now they've stood in front of a room and pitched their own idea in 60 seconds - something most adults have never done.

That is the reframe to leave them with. A pitch isn't a performance you survive. It is a conversation you start, on purpose, and it gets easier every time you run it.
