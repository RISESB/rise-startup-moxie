# Creating Personas + Calculating Total Addressable Market (TAM)

_Most students describe their customer as "teenagers" and their market as "everyone." Week 10 kills both. They give the customer a name and a face, then learn to size the opportunity honestly with three rings instead of one big number._

Ask a student who their customer is and you almost always get the same answer. "Teenagers." "Small businesses." "People who like fitness." It sounds like an answer. It is really a way of avoiding one.

The problem with "everyone" is that you cannot build for everyone, find everyone, or say anything that lands with everyone. A target that big is the same as no target at all.

So Week 10 does two jobs. Students get specific about one real person, then they get honest about how many people like that actually exist. The guiding question runs both at once: who exactly is your customer, and how many of them are there?

## Give the customer a face

We start by drawing a line between two things students tend to blur. There is a difference between knowing who your customer is and understanding them.

A persona forces the second one. It gives your customer a name, a daily life, and a problem you can picture. On Monday we build one out loud, layer by layer.

- **Demographics** - name, age, location, job, income, family situation.
- **Goals** - what they are trying to achieve in the area your business touches.
- **Frustrations** - what is getting in their way right now.
- **Behaviors** - how they currently solve the problem, usually badly.
- **Watering holes** - where they spend time, online and off.
- **Quote** - one sentence they might actually say about the problem.

Then we meet Maria. She is 34, lives in South Bend, teaches preschool, makes $38,000 a year. She wants to feed her family real food on a budget without losing her whole Sunday to cooking. She buys frozen meals she hates because the store is 20 minutes away and she has no time to plan.

Her quote is the tell: "I just want food that's real and doesn't cost me an hour every night."

Once Maria is that clear, I know what to build, where to find her, and what to say when I do. "Teenagers" gives you none of that. Maria gives you all three.

## Three rings, not one big number

The second half of Monday is the part students inflate the most, so we slow it down. Market size is not one number. It is three rings, each smaller and more honest than the last.

> TAM is everyone who could buy - the full pool. SAM is who you can actually reach right now - the pool you can swim in. SOM is the realistic slice you capture in year one to three - the pool you will actually swim in.

Back to Maria's world to make it concrete. TAM is all budget-conscious families in the US who want meal-prep help, roughly a $50 billion market. SAM is families in South Bend you can reach through local channels, maybe 15,000 households. SOM is what you can realistically serve in year one, starting at 20 and growing toward 100.

Here is the line I want students to carry out of the room. Investors want a big TAM because it shows the opportunity is real. But they believe your SOM, because that is where your credibility lives.

A defendable SOM you can explain beats an inflated TAM you cannot. Students reach for the biggest number they can find. The work of the week is teaching them the small number is the one that earns trust.

## How we actually run it

Monday is lecture and a first persona draft. Everyone fills out every section and gives their customer a name. When I walk the room I do two things: I find the most specific detail on each sheet and reinforce it, and I find the vaguest one and push. "What does this person actually do when the problem hits?"

Then pairs read each other's personas and answer one question. Does this feel like a real person? If it does not, it is not done.

Tuesday is where the numbers get real. We use AI as a research partner, not an answer machine. Students ask what data sources would give real market numbers - census data, industry reports, local statistics - and go find them instead of guessing. Every number gets documented with its source: TAM with a source, SAM with reasoning, SOM with a rationale. Those go straight into the business portfolio, because they feed the pitch later.

Friday we pressure-test it with a market-sizing Founder Fight. A business for high schoolers only versus one for everyone under 25. A local South Bend shop versus the same thing scaled nationally from day one. A niche product for competitive athletes versus general fitness for everyone. Then the debrief question that does the teaching: which has the bigger TAM, which has the more realistic SOM, and are those even the same business?

## Where students get stuck

The common trap is the vanity TAM. A student finds a $50 billion number, writes it down, and thinks the work is done. So in the 1-on-1s I stay on one prompt: walk me through your numbers, where did you get them, and why is your SOM realistic?

The other snag is the persona that drifts from the plan. If their persona is a busy working parent but their May-B-P is aimed at classmates, something is off. I ask it plainly. Does your persona match who your May-B-P actually reaches? If your May-B-P reaches ten people, are they the right ten?

That is the quiet point of the whole week. A persona and a market size are not decoration for the pitch deck. They are a check on whether you are building for someone real and reachable, or for a crowd that only exists in your head.

## What good looks like by Friday

By the end of the week a student should be able to name one specific person their business is for, out loud, without reaching for "teenagers." Maria, not a demographic.

They should have three market numbers, each with a source or a reason, and they should be able to defend the smallest one - the SOM - as the number that is actually theirs to go get.

And they should feel the connection to their own plan. Their persona should match their May-B-P target, and if it does not, they should know that is the thing to fix next.

Next week they stop researching customers and go talk to them. One real conversation teaches more than a week of estimates. But it lands better when they already know exactly who they are looking for.
