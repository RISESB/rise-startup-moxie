# AI Basics and Outreach

_Most students have used AI to skip work. This week we teach the opposite move: using Claude and ChatGPT to think harder, research faster, and send a real outreach message they actually stand behind._

Ask a room of teenagers how they have used ChatGPT or Claude and the answers come back fast. Homework. Essays. Looking things up the night before a test.

That is one use case, and it is the one we are least interested in. Week 5 takes AI out of the homework-shortcut box and puts it where entrepreneurs use it, which is a very different place.

The frame I give students on Monday is simple. AI is not going to replace entrepreneurs. But entrepreneurs who know how to use it will outcompete the ones who don't. The question was never whether to use it. It is how to use it well.

## Tool, not crutch

The core reframe of the week fits in one line, and we put it on the board and sit with it.

> AI is a bionic arm, not a robot. It extends what you can do. It does not do the thinking for you.

That distinction is the whole subject. A crutch does the work so you don't have to. A tool makes your own work faster and sharper. We want students leaving able to feel the difference in their own hands.

To make it concrete, we get honest about what these tools are. Claude and ChatGPT are prediction engines. They guess the most likely next word based on patterns in text. That makes them useful for some things and completely unreliable for others.

**AI is good at:**

- **Drafting** - emails, pitches, descriptions, outlines.
- **Research summaries** - explaining a concept or comparing options.
- **Brainstorming** - surfacing options you would not have thought of.
- **Stress-testing** - poking holes in your plan and finding the weak spots.

**AI is not good at:**

- **Current information** - it has a knowledge cutoff and does not know today.
- **Your specific market or customers** - it has never met them.
- **Deciding for you** - it will sound confident even when it is wrong.
- **The real conversations** - it cannot replace talking to an actual customer.

## The rules real entrepreneurs follow

We give students three rules, and I am careful to tell them these are not school rules. They are the rules working professionals actually use.

- **Rule 1: Bionic arm, not a robot.** You do the thinking. AI helps you execute faster.
- **Rule 2: Focus on problems.** Use AI to go deeper on a real problem, not to shortcut the work of understanding it.
- **Rule 3: Stay safe.** Do not paste sensitive information, personal data, or unreleased plans into a free tool. Use a business account when you can.

We also name the two tools we use all year, because students ask. ChatGPT tends to be strong at brainstorming and structured output, with more tutorials floating around. Claude tends to be strong at nuanced writing and following complex instructions, and often sounds more human. For most tasks either one works. When in doubt, run the same prompt in both and compare.

## Prompting is the skill

Here is the part that separates a crutch from a tool, and it is the most practical thing we teach all week. The output you get is only as good as the prompt you give. Garbage in, garbage out.

A strong prompt has three parts:

- **Context** - who you are and what situation you are in.
- **Task** - specifically what you want it to do.
- **Format** - how you want the answer to come back.

I demo this live because the gap is hard to believe until you see it. First I type the bad prompt: "Write me an email to a potential customer." The output is generic and stiff.

Then I type the good one: "I am a 17-year-old starting a lawn care business in South Bend, Indiana. I want to reach out to homeowners in my neighborhood who might need weekly maintenance. Write me a short, friendly cold email, no more than five sentences, that sounds like a real person, not a corporation."

The difference is night and day, and the students see it land in real time. Then I ask them what made the second one better, and they tell me. That is the lesson teaching itself.

## Where students get stuck

The biggest trap is trusting the first draft. AI writes with confidence, so a generic answer can feel finished when it is not. This is where I walk the room hardest.

In the live practice block, students point the tool at their own May-B-P concept. Explain the idea back to me as a customer, not a business person. Find three weaknesses in this concept. Suggest three specific first customers. After each one, the question is always the same: what did it get right, and what did it miss?

The second trap shows up on Tuesday in the outreach workshop. Students use AI to research their market and draft a real message to a real potential customer. Then pairs read the drafts out loud, and I ask one blunt question: does this sound like the person sitting next to you, or does it sound like a press release?

The revising is the point. AI gives you a fast starting point. Making it true and making it sound like you is the part that was always yours to do.

## What good looks like by Friday

By the end of the week I am not looking for polish. I am looking for a student who can tell me, in their own words, where AI helped and where it fell short.

Concretely, good looks like this. Every student has sent one real outreach message, or scheduled it to send, to an actual person. They can name one thing the AI got wrong about their business. And they can finish this sentence: "One thing I will use AI for every week going forward is ______."

The reframe to leave them with is the one we opened on. AI levels the field. A teenager with a laptop and a good prompt can research, draft, and reach out at a level that used to take a team. The thinking still has to be theirs. That is the difference between a tool and a crutch, and this is the week they learn to feel it.
