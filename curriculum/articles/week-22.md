# CRM + Outreach Strategies

_Most sales are not lost to a no. They are lost because nobody followed up. Week 22 hands students a simple tracker and a way to follow up that earns trust instead of annoyance._

Ask a student what kills a sale and they will usually say the customer said no. Sometimes that is true. Most of the time it is quieter than that. The customer showed interest, meant to decide, got busy, and never heard from anyone again.

That is the reframe we open the week with. The money is not in the pitch. It is in the follow-up. A lead who liked you and then forgot about you is not a rejection, it is a message you never sent.

So Week 22 does two things. It teaches students to track the people who show interest, and it teaches them to follow up in a way that feels like a gift rather than a nag.

## A CRM is a spreadsheet, not fancy software

First we get the word off its pedestal. CRM stands for Customer Relationship Management. Big companies pay for expensive tools to do it. A student needs a spreadsheet, and that is genuinely it.

What matters is not the software, it is the columns. For each person, a working CRM tracks:

- **Name and contact info** - who they are and how to reach them.
- **How you met** - where they came from, so you learn which channels actually bring people in.
- **Their stage** - lead, prospect, customer, or repeat.
- **Last contact** - the date and what was said.
- **Next action and date** - the one column that does the real work.

That last column is the whole point. Next Action is what keeps a person from falling through the cracks. A CRM without it is just a list of names you feel vaguely guilty about.

## People move through stages, left to right

Once the tracker is up, we give students a way to see it. A relationship is not on or off. It moves.

> Lead (fits your segment) → Prospect (showed interest) → Customer (bought once) → Repeat or Referrer (buys again, tells others).

Your CRM exists to move people from left to right. That is the job. When you look at the sheet, you are asking one question about each person: what is the next thing that nudges them one stage over?

This tends to change how students see their own numbers. Ten names feel like nothing until you sort them by stage and notice three are prospects sitting one message away from buying.

## The follow-up game: give three times before you ask

Here is where most people get follow-up wrong, and where we spend real time. Bad follow-up asks the same empty question over and over. "Just checking in! Any thoughts??" three times in a row is not persistence, it is pressure.

Good follow-up gives value every time. Each message hands the person something - a helpful tip, a story, a reminder of the benefit, a small offer. The ask, when it comes, is easy to say yes to.

We teach it as a rule of thumb: give three times before you ask again, and always make the next step easy. Compare the two side by side and students feel the difference immediately.

- **Bad:** "Just checking in! Any thoughts??"
- **Good:** "Saw this and thought of our conversation. Here's the useful thing. Whenever you're ready, here's the link."

On Tuesday students turn this into a real sequence. Four messages for one warm lead: thank and connect with no ask, give value, give value with a soft invite, then a clear and easy ask. They use AI to draft it fast, then edit until it sounds like a human wrote it. Pairs read each other's and answer one question: would this annoy you, or help you?

## Where students get stuck

Two places, every time.

The first is the empty Next Action column. A student enters five names and leaves the most important column blank, which turns the CRM back into a dead list. So we walk the room asking one thing: who is sitting in your CRM with no next action, and what should it be? The tracker only works if every row points forward.

The second is the ask. Left alone, students either never ask or lead with the ask and skip the giving. The three-gives-before-an-ask rule fixes both. It gives shy students permission to reach out, because the first messages are just generous, and it slows down the pushy ones, because they cannot open with "so are you buying or not?"

Thursday is where it gets real. Students stop drafting and actually send follow-up messages to real people in their CRM, then log each one. The 1-on-1 question is plain: how many did you send today, and what is your reply rate so far? Numbers beat opinions about whether outreach is working.

## What good looks like by Friday

By Friday, a student should be able to pull up their CRM and walk you through it. Not a perfect one. A real one, with every current lead and customer in it, a stage on each, and a next action with a date on the ones that matter.

They should be able to point to their hottest lead and tell you the exact next move and when it happens. And they should have sent at least a few real messages into the world and gotten at least one number back, even a small and messy one.

We close with the Founder Fight, relationship edition, because the argument is worth having out loud: ten loyal customers or a hundred one-time buyers? A founder who tracks every lead or one who keeps it all in their head? Students usually arrive at the answer themselves, which is the point.

The reframe to leave them with is simple. Interest is not the finish line, it is the start of a relationship you are responsible for keeping alive. Track the people, follow up with something worth reading, and never let a warm lead go cold because you forgot they existed.
