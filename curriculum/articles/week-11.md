# Customer Discovery Interviews + Target Market Definition

_Most customer conversations lie to you, and they do it politely. Week 11 teaches students to ask the kind of questions that get an honest answer, then go get two of them from real people._

Ask a student to go validate their idea and watch what they do. They describe the thing they want to build, in glowing detail, to someone who likes them, and walk away convinced they are onto something.

They are not onto something. They just got lied to. Not maliciously. The person across the table wanted to be nice, so they said "great idea" and moved on.

Week 11 breaks that trap. The guiding question we put in front of students is simple: what do your potential customers actually experience, and is it what you assumed? Almost always, it is not.

## People lie, so stop asking about your idea

The frame we teach comes from a book called *The Mom Test* by Rob Fitzpatrick. The title says it all. Even your mom will tell you your idea is great, so her opinion is worthless as data. If you cannot trust your own mother for a straight answer about your business, you cannot trust a stranger for one either.

The fix is the whole method in one line:

> Don't ask people whether your idea is good. Ask about their life, and let the truth show up on its own.

There are three rules, and we keep them on the board all week:

- **Talk about their life, not your idea.** Ask about their experience with the problem, not their opinion of your solution.
- **Ask about specifics in the past, not hypotheticals about the future.** "Have you ever...?" beats "Would you ever...?" every time.
- **Talk less, listen more.** Your job in the room is to understand, not to pitch.

That last one is the hardest for a teenager who is excited about what they are building. So we name it out loud and hold them to it.

## Good questions, bad questions

The skill here is not confidence. It is the wording of the question, and small changes flip one from useless to useful.

Here is what we tell them not to ask:

- "Do you think this is a good idea?"
- "Would you use this if I built it?"
- "How much would you pay for something like this?"

Every one invites a polite lie. They ask the person to imagine a future and be encouraging about it, which is exactly what people are good at faking.

Here is what we teach instead:

- **"Tell me about the last time you dealt with the problem. What happened?"** - a real story, not a guess.
- **"What do you currently do about it? How is that working?"** - their actual behavior today.
- **"What is the hardest part of the situation for you?"** - where the pain actually sits.
- **"Have you ever looked for a solution? What did you find?"** - whether they care enough to have searched.

The move underneath all of them is the same. Get the person talking about their past, and you get behavior instead of flattery.

## What to listen for once they are talking

Asking a good question is only half of it. Most students hear "yes, that's annoying" and write down a win. We slow them down and teach what actually counts as signal.

- **Pain.** How much does this problem bother them? Do they lose sleep over it, or is it mildly annoying? Mild annoyance does not build a business.
- **Workarounds.** What do they do about it now? A messy, expensive, duct-taped workaround is one of the strongest signals you can get, because the pain was real enough to make them act.
- **Budget signals.** Do they already spend money on this? What someone already pays for tells you more than any answer to "would you pay" ever will.

I keep coming back to that last one, because it reframes pricing before we ever teach pricing. A customer's existing behavior is worth more than their stated intention.

## How we actually run it

Monday is the lecture and the interview design. Every student writes a 5-question interview, and the questions have to clear three bars: ask about past experience, focus on the problem and not the solution, and stay open-ended. Then I walk the room and stop at each student: "Read me question three. Does it pass the Mom Test?" If it does not, we rewrite it on the spot. That single pass is where most of the learning happens.

Tuesday is role play. Pairs, one interviewer and one customer, a full run through the five questions, then switch. The facilitator catches the moment a student slips into pitching and stops them cold: "That was a pitch. What could you ask instead?"

Then they schedule the live version. The bar for the week is at least two real conversations with people who match their persona. Not surveys. Not friends. Strangers or acquaintances who actually have the problem, the early adopters who feel it most.

## Where students get stuck

Two places, every year.

The first is interviewing their friends. Friends are easy to book and useless as data, because they are the people most likely to be nice. We push students toward people who will tell them something they do not want to hear.

The second is pitching mid-interview. The customer says something, the student gets excited, and suddenly they are selling instead of listening. The interview collapses into a sales call and the data is gone. Naming it ahead of time helps, but they still do it, so we catch it live in role play until the habit sticks.

The point of the week is not to confirm they were right. It is to find where they were wrong. On Thursday and Friday, students update their persona and their May-B-P against what they actually heard, and the good ones come back a little humbled.

## What good looks like by Friday

By Friday, a student should be able to name one thing they assumed about their customer that turned out to be wrong. If everything they heard confirmed what they already believed, they either talked to the wrong people or they were still pitching.

They should have two real conversations behind them, exact quotes where they could catch them, and a persona that changed at least a little because of what a stranger told them.

The reframe to leave them with carries into the build weeks ahead. You do not get to decide what your customer needs. You go find out, and you let what they actually do outvote what you hoped was true.
