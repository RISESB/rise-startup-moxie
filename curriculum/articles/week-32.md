# Team Roles + Operational Planning

_Most student businesses do not stall because the idea was bad. They stall because nobody planned the actual work. Week 32 fixes both problems at once: who does what, and when it gets done._

Ask a student how their business is going and you often get a shrug and a story about being busy. Busy is not the same as moving. Busy usually means a founder doing a dozen jobs at once, badly, with no plan for any of them.

By Week 32 the business is real. There is a product, some marketing, maybe a little money changing hands. So we stop talking about the idea and start talking about the machine that runs it: the roles the work needs, and the plan that gets the work done.

The quiet argument underneath the week: a good business is not one heroic founder grinding through everything. It is a set of jobs, covered on purpose, on a schedule you can actually keep.

## From three roles to four functions

Back in Week 9, students learned every business needs a Builder, a Seller, and an Operator. That was the starter version. Now that the business is live, we get specific about the jobs that actually need doing.

Every business, even a solo one, has to cover four functions:

- **Product/Operations** - making and delivering the thing.
- **Sales/Marketing** - finding customers and closing them.
- **Finance/Admin** - money, records, logistics.
- **Leadership** - vision, decisions, keeping it all moving.

If you are solo, you wear all four hats. That is fine. The point is to know which hat you are wearing at any given moment, and to be honest about the one you are weakest in.

Naming the four functions does something for students. Work that felt like one giant undefined pile turns into four labeled buckets. You cannot plan a pile.

## The founder trap

Here is the trap we name out loud, because most of them are already walking into it. The biggest way a business stalls is the founder trying to do everything, including the parts they are bad at or quietly hate. It feels responsible. It is actually the fastest way to grind to a stop.

> Great founders do what only they can do, and get help with the rest.

The reframe I want students to sit with: help does not mean employees. Most of them hear "get help" and picture a payroll they cannot afford, so they never ask for any.

Help has many forms. A friend who is good at the thing you avoid. A freelancer for a few hours. A family member. A tool. Automation. The question is not "can I afford to hire someone," it is "what is the cheapest way to get this job off my plate so I can do the work only I can do."

## Rhythm beats willpower

The second half of the week is planning, and this is where most student businesses actually break.

An operating plan answers three plain questions: what work happens, when, and who does it. Most businesses at this level do not fail from a bad idea. They fail because things just quietly do not get done.

A simple weekly rhythm looks like this:

- **Make** - a set time to prepare product or deliver the service.
- **Market** - a set time for outreach and marketing.
- **Money** - a set time to handle admin and finances.
- **Review** - a short weekly check: what got done, what is next.

The line I keep repeating: rhythm beats willpower. When the work has a scheduled home on the calendar, it happens. When it lives in your head as "I should get to that," it does not.

I am blunt with them about their real lives too. School, sports, a job, a social life. A plan built for an imaginary founder with eight free hours a day is worse than no plan. Build for the schedule you actually have.

## How we teach it: the role map, then the plan

We run this as two hands-on passes, not a lecture they nod through.

First, the **role map**. Students list every role their business needs, then mark each one three ways: fill it well, fill it poorly, or need help. We pull the Week 9 team map back out and the Week 30 SOPs, because a gap you documented an SOP for is a gap you can hand to someone else.

As they work, I walk the room asking one question: "What is the role you are worst at, and who or what could cover it?" That single question does more than the slides do.

Second, the **weekly operating plan**. Students build one real week: what work happens on which days, roughly how long, and how they will know it got done.

Wednesday's speaker gets pointed at the same idea. We prep students to ask how the speaker decides what to do themselves versus what they hand off.

## Where students get stuck

Two places, every year.

The first is pride. A student marks every role as "fill it well" because admitting a weakness feels like admitting failure. That map is useless. The whole value is in the honest "I am bad at this" boxes, so I push until at least one shows up.

The second is fantasy planning. They build a beautiful week that assumes they are a different person with more discipline and more time. So Friday's 1-on-1 asks the real question: what is the first thing that will get skipped, and how will you protect it?

Friday also runs a team-and-operations "Deal or No Deal." A skilled friend handles all your marketing for 20% of profit. A tool automates your admin for $25 a month and gives you back four hours. Deal or no deal? There is no clean answer. The point is to make them price their own time out loud, which most of them have never once done.

## What good looks like by Friday

By the end of the week, a student should walk out with two things in their portfolio. A role map that honestly shows which functions they cover well, which they cover badly, and a real plan for the gaps. And a one-week operating rhythm they could start Monday without lying to themselves about their schedule.

If they can also name the first job they would hand off the moment they can, and say what four hours a week is worth to them, they are in good shape.

The reframe to leave them with is small and durable. You are not the business. You are one role in it, plus a plan for the rest.
