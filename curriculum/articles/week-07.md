# Matchmaking + Walk, Talk, Show Up

_Students spent Unit 1 finding a problem. This week we go back to Walk, Talk, Show Up with a new job: find the people and resources that make the idea real. Entrepreneurship is not a solo sport._

Ask a student who they need to build their business and most will say the same thing. Customers. People who will pay.

That answer is not wrong. It is just short. Customers are one ring of a much bigger circle, and a founder who only thinks about customers is trying to build alone in a world that rewards company.

So Week 7 does one thing. We take students back to Walk, Talk, Show Up, the discovery habit from Unit 1, and we point it at people instead of problems. Same three moves, new lens.

## You do not build alone

The reframe we open with is blunt. Entrepreneurship is not a solo sport. You build it with an ecosystem.

Here is the sentence we put on the board and sit with:

> An entrepreneurial ecosystem is the network of people, organizations, and resources that support entrepreneurs in a given community.

Then we draw it. A student in the center, five rings around them:

- **Mentors** - people who have done what you want to do.
- **Peers** - other entrepreneurs at your stage.
- **Customers** - the people whose problem you are solving.
- **Resources** - money, tools, spaces, programs.
- **Champions** - people who will open doors for you.

Most people only think about that middle ring. The stronger move is to invest in all five, because each one speeds up the next. A mentor saves you a year of mistakes. A champion gets you in a room you could not enter on your own.

## Matchmaking is a decision, not luck

Once the rings are on the board, we name the skill that fills them. We call it matchmaking.

Matchmaking is the process of intentionally connecting yourself with the right people at the right time, instead of waiting for it to happen. It is a decision you make, not luck you wait for.

Three questions drive it:

- Who has solved a version of my problem before?
- Who knows my potential customer better than I do?
- Who has resources or access that I do not have yet?

Answer those and you have a short list of people you need to find. But there is a catch we say out loud every time. When you find them, show up with something to offer, not just something to ask for. A student who only takes does not get a second conversation.

## Same three moves, new lens

This is where Walk, Talk, Show Up comes back. In Unit 1 it was how students found problems. Now it is how they find people.

- **Walk** - move through the spaces where your ecosystem already exists. Farmers markets, business districts, community events, makerspaces.
- **Talk** - have real conversations. Not pitching. Asking. "How did you start? What do you wish you had known?"
- **Show Up** - be present where your ecosystem gathers. Volunteer. Contribute. Be seen before you need anything.

The activity that makes it concrete is the ecosystem map. Students draw themselves in the center, the five rings around them, and fill in what they already know. Then they circle the three biggest gaps. Those three gaps are their matchmaking targets, the people they most need to connect with right now.

Tuesday we push for specifics. Every entry has to have a name or a real description. Not "a mentor" but "someone who runs a food business in South Bend." Then students go back to their Discovery Tracker from Unit 1, add one column, "Who should I talk to about this?", and reconnect every strong observation to a real person they could reach.

## Where students get stuck

The most common stall is the map that stays vague. A student writes "a mentor" in the ring and feels done. They are not. A ring you cannot name is a ring you cannot act on, so we walk the room pushing one question: who, specifically, and how would you show up for them first?

The second stall is the ask. Students want to reach out and immediately request something, because that is what "networking" looks like to them. We slow that down. Using the outreach skills from Week 5, each student drafts one message to one target. AI helps with the first draft. Then they edit until it sounds like them and until it offers before it asks.

The third stall is quieter. Some students genuinely believe they have no ecosystem yet. The pair share is built for exactly this. Trade maps with a partner and you almost always find that their circle has something yours is missing, and that you can connect them to someone too. The ecosystem is bigger than any one student can see from the center of it.

The Wednesday speaker and the Thursday thank-you cards feed the same muscle. After the speaker we ask which ring that person represented, whether they belong on someone's map. The thank-you cards are handwritten and specific: one thing you heard, one question it sparked, one genuine thank you. That is Show Up in practice, and it is how a one-time speaker becomes a real connection.

## What good looks like by Friday

By Friday you are not looking for a finished network. You are looking for a map with names on it and a plan to act.

A student in a good spot can show you their ecosystem map with real people or specific descriptions in the rings, point to their three matchmaking targets, and tell you which one they are reaching out to this week and what they will say. Better still, some have already sent the message.

We close the week the way we always do, with each student saying one concrete thing out loud. Here it is a name and a next step: who they will connect with before next Friday, and how that person ties back to their May-B-P.

The reframe to leave them with is the one we opened on. You are not doing this alone, and you do not have to wait for the right people to show up. You go find them, you bring something, and you build the circle on purpose.
