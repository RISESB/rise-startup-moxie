# AI Campaigns + A/B Testing

_By now students can make one marketing asset. This week they build a whole campaign with AI in an afternoon, then stop guessing which version works and let the data tell them._

There is a moment in most marketing that quietly decides everything, and almost nobody notices it. You post something, it does okay or it doesn't, and you move on to the next thing with no idea why. You were hoping. Week 23 is about the difference between hoping and knowing.

Two things happen this week. Students go from making one piece to running a coordinated campaign, and they learn to test versions instead of arguing about them. Both are skills that used to take a marketing team. AI put them in reach of a teenager with a laptop.

## A campaign is a planned push, not a random post

First we get the word straight. A campaign is a set of coordinated pieces, aimed at one goal, over a set period of time. Not one post you fired off because you had a free afternoon. A planned push where every piece points the same direction.

The version we teach students to build has four parts:

- **One goal** - what you actually want to happen.
- **One segment** - who you are talking to.
- **One offer** - the thing you are asking them to take.
- **Three to five pieces** - posts, a short video, an email - that all point to the same next step.

That last part is where beginners leak. Five pieces that each ask for something different is not a campaign, it is noise. Five pieces pointing at one next step is a machine.

## Let AI draft, you direct

This is the week AI earns its keep, because building five coordinated pieces by hand is slow and building them with AI is fast. The trick is that you give the strategy and AI gives the draft. You already know your segment, your offer, and your brand voice from earlier weeks, so you hand those over and let it do the typing.

We teach a fill-in-the-blank prompt:

> I run [business] for [segment]. My brand voice is [three words]. I want a one-week campaign to get [goal]. Draft three social captions, one short video script, and one email, all pointing to [offer or next step].

The draft lands in seconds. Then the real job starts, and it is the job I keep repeating all year: make it true and make it sound like you. AI gives you a fast starting point, not a finished product. A student who ships the raw output has skipped the only part that was theirs to do.

## A/B testing: let the data pick

Here is the part that changes how students think. A/B testing means you show two versions, change exactly one thing, and keep whichever performs better. Version A and version B, one difference between them, and the data settles the argument instead of the loudest opinion in the room.

Things worth testing:

- The headline or hook, meaning the first line
- The image or thumbnail
- The call to action ("Buy now" versus "Try free")
- The price framing or the offer

And one rule that matters more than the rest: change one thing at a time. If you swap the image and the caption at once and version B wins, you have learned nothing, because you cannot say which change did it. One variable, or the test is worthless.

The best part for a classroom with no budget: you do not need software. Post version A on Monday, post version B on Wednesday at the same time, and compare the views, clicks, or replies. That is a real test. Students are often surprised that the thing that felt fancy and out of reach is this plain.

## Why this belongs in the year, not just a marketing unit

It would be easy to file this under "marketing tactics" and move on. I think it is bigger than that. A/B testing is the OODA Loop from Week 1 wearing marketing clothes. Observe what happened, orient on what it means, decide what to keep, act, and loop. We are not teaching students a growth hack. We are teaching them to treat their own decisions as experiments, which is a habit that outlasts any single platform or tool.

There is also a real conversation to have about judgment, and we do not dodge it. On Friday we run marketing "deal or no deal" scenarios, and one of them is pointed: your A/B test shows version B doubles your clicks, but B is a little off-brand and a little clickbaity. Do you run it? There is no clean answer, and that is the point. The data tells you what works. It does not tell you what you are willing to become to get it. Students should feel that tension, not resolve it too quickly.

## What "good" looks like this week

By Friday, a student should have a campaign kit sitting in their portfolio - a handful of coordinated pieces, all in their own voice, all pointing at one next step. They should be able to say, in one sentence, what they are testing and how they will read the result. And they should have at least one early number, even a small and messy one, that they got from the world instead of from their own head.

The reframe to leave them with is simple. You do not have to guess anymore. You can build fast, put two versions in front of real people, and let the results make the call. Test to know, not to hope.
