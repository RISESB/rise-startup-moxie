# Customer Segmentation + Messaging

_Most students think a bigger audience is a better one. Week 19 flips that. The move is to talk to one specific person about their specific problem, which is the only way anyone leans in._

Ask a student who their customer is and you can almost predict the answer. "Everyone." Or the slightly more polished version, "anyone who needs this."

It sounds ambitious. It feels safe. And it is the fastest way to fail at marketing, because when you talk to everyone you sound like nothing.

So Week 19 does one job. We take "everyone" apart and teach students to aim a specific message at a specific person, which is the only kind of message people actually hear.

The guiding question we put in front of them all week is blunt on purpose: not everyone is your customer, so who exactly are you talking to, and what do you say to them?

## Talk to everyone, reach no one

We open with the reframe because it runs against every instinct a beginner has.

A megaphone pointed at an empty field is loud and lands on nobody. The same message aimed at one person who has the problem gets a lean-in. Bigger is not better. Sharper is better.

That is the whole argument of the week compressed into one image, and students feel it before they can explain it.

## What segmentation actually means

Once the reframe is on the table, we get the definition straight:

> Segmentation means breaking a big market into smaller groups that share something in common, so you can serve one group really well instead of all groups poorly.

Then we hand them the common ways to cut a market, because "smaller group" is too vague to act on:

- **Demographic** - age, income, location, job, family stage
- **Behavioral** - how they buy, how often, what triggers them
- **Needs-based** - what problem they are trying to solve
- **Psychographic** - values, lifestyle, identity

The example that makes it click is a meal-kit business. Same box on the porch, but busy working parents, college students, and seniors who can't cook easily are three different people. One product, three very different messages. Segmentation is just admitting that out loud.

## Picking one segment first

This is where students want to hedge, so we make the choice concrete. You pick ONE segment to start, and the best first segment sits where three things overlap:

- **Highest pain** - they feel the problem most
- **Easiest to reach** - you already know where they are
- **Ability to pay** - they have money for a solution

I say the same line every year because students need to hear it: starting narrow is not thinking small. Amazon started with only books. Facebook started with only Harvard students. Narrow first, then expand.

The activity that makes it real is a scoring pass. Students list every possible segment for their business, then score each one on pain, reach, and pay, and circle a primary. When I walk the room I ask one question that usually exposes a fake answer: could you reach 10 of these people this month? If they can't, the segment is a wish, not a market.

## Message-market fit

A segment without a matching message is only half the work, so the back half of the week is writing.

Once you know who you are talking to, the message has to name their problem, show you understand it, and offer relief, in their own words. We teach it as a formula so students have something to run, not just admire.

Call out the segment, name their specific pain, show the outcome, give one clear next step. Four moves, in order.

The model I put on the board: "Busy parents in South Bend: dinner shouldn't cost you your whole evening. Fresh local meal kits, on your porch, ready in 20 minutes. Order your first week free." You can hear who it is for and what to do next, and that is the entire test.

Tuesday students build a one-page profile of their primary segment - where they spend time, what they already buy, what words they use for the problem, who they trust. Then they write three versions of their core message and workshop them in pairs, where the partner plays the segment and calls out any word that feels off. AI helps here too, mostly for pulling the actual phrases a segment uses, but the student still decides which version is true.

## Where students get stuck

Two places, every year.

The first is the student who narrows in name only. They say "small business owners" and think they have a segment, when that is still a stadium of different people. The fix is the reach test - name 10 real ones you could find this month - which turns a category into an actual group of humans.

The second is messaging written in the founder's words instead of the customer's. It describes the product's features and forgets the person's pain. My whole 1-on-1 focus on Friday is one prompt: say your message out loud, tell me who it is for and what you want them to do. If they can't answer cleanly, the message is not done yet.

## What good looks like by Friday

By Friday you are not grading polish. You are listening for focus.

A student who is exactly where they need to be can name one primary segment and say why they chose them first, can say their strongest message in one breath, and that message uses the customer's words, not their own.

We close the week the way we close every week, with each student saying the thing out loud. Here it is their core message in a single sentence, aimed at one person, with one next step. If the room can tell who it is for without being told, the message is working.

Everything downstream this year - the brand story, the campaigns, the pitch - gets sharper when the target is one person instead of everyone. Narrow is not small. Narrow is how you finally get heard.
