# Reflection + 2-Minute Pitch

_The Unit 2 final is not a test you study for. It is a 2-minute pitch and an 8-slide deck, built to a real competition standard, delivered to a room acting as investors._

Most school finals ask a student to prove they remember something. This one asks them to prove they can carry an idea into a room and defend it out loud.

That is a different skill, and it is the one that actually matters. By Week 17 students have matched a customer, tested assumptions, and made something real. This week they have to stand up and say what it is in two minutes.

So the Unit 2 final is not a worksheet. It is a pitch. We hold it to the same format real founders use, and we tell students that on Monday so they know the bar is not made up.

## The standard is real, so we borrow a real one

We build the deck to the Notre Dame McCloskey Business Plan Competition Round 2 structure. That is the frame for the whole week.

I like using a real competition standard because it removes the argument. Nobody can say "this is just a school thing" when the format comes from an event founders actually compete in. The standard is the standard.

Every student builds the same eight slides:

- **Slide 1: Cover** - business name, tagline, founder name.
- **Slide 2: Problem** - a specific problem, a specific customer, why it matters now.
- **Slide 3: Solution** - what you offer, why it is different, one proof point.
- **Slide 4: Market** - who your ideal customer is and how many exist (TAM/SAM/SOM or a realistic local estimate).
- **Slide 5: Business Model** - revenue streams, price point, how you make money.
- **Slide 6: Traction** - sales made, customers interviewed, tests run, partnerships formed.
- **Slide 7: Financials** - price, estimated monthly revenue, costs, path to profit.
- **Slide 8: The Ask** - what you need next, plus a one-line vision.

Eight slides, two minutes, then two minutes of questions from the class. The deck stays on screen and students present from it. No reading off a phone.

## How the week is built

The mistake would be to give students the format on Monday and the stage on Tuesday. We spread it across the week so the deck gets built, then reviewed, then rehearsed.

- **Monday** - build slides 1 through 4. Cover, Problem, Solution, Market. Rough is fine.
- **Tuesday** - build slides 5 through 8. Business Model, Traction, Financials, Ask. By end of day every slide has content, even if messy.
- **Wednesday** - full deck review and refinement. Peer feedback, then revision.
- **Thursday** - practice pitches. Every student delivers the full 2 minutes at least once.
- **Friday** - final pitches to the class.

The build days run the same way. Students work, the facilitator circulates with coaching questions, and the questions do most of the teaching.

On the Problem slide you ask: who specifically has this, and what evidence do you have? On Financials you ask them to show the math, price times units. The coaching is not "make it prettier." It is "make it true."

> Numbers are more convincing than stories. "I sold 12 units" beats "people really like it."

That line is worth repeating at every desk. Traction is where students want to wave their hands, and the whole week pushes against that.

## Where students get stuck

Three slides break down more than the others, so watch for them Wednesday during revision.

The Traction slide with no real numbers. Push these students to get at least one real data point before Thursday, even a small one.

The Financials slide with no actual math. Make them calculate it, roughly if they must, but calculate it. A revenue number with no arithmetic behind it falls apart the second a classmate asks about it.

The Problem slide that is too vague. "Everyone" is not a customer. Push for a specific person with a specific pain.

The other place students freeze is the pitch itself, not the deck. On Thursday you will see students go over time, rush through slides, or lock up during Q&A. Those are separate problems from a weak deck, and they get fixed by reps, not edits. That is why everyone pitches at least once before Friday.

## What good looks like by Friday

By Friday the room becomes a pitch space. Chairs in an arc, screen up, class acting as an investor panel. You keep time and signal at 90 seconds, cut off firmly at two minutes.

A student who is where they need to be can stand up, get through eight slides in two minutes without reading, and take a real question without falling apart. The deck is clean enough to follow and honest enough to defend.

You will know the Q&A is working when the class asks the sharp questions on its own. If they go quiet, you have a few ready: who is your first customer, what would make you quit this business, what is the one thing that has to be true for this to work.

Then you close Unit 2 on energy, not evaluation. Take ten minutes for shoutouts, each student naming one thing a classmate did well, about the business or the pitch or their growth. There is no game this week. The pitches were the main event.

The reframe to leave them with is the one the whole unit was pointing at. You went from an idea to something you could stand behind in front of a room. That is what a founder does. Unit 3 is about getting more customers to find you, and we start Monday.
