# The End Part 1

_The finale is not a new project. It is the moment students stop building and start packaging everything they already made into one clear picture a stranger can trust._

For most of the year we told students to keep making things. Interviews, prototypes, prices, a brand, a pitch. Pile it up. Get it wrong. Fix it.

Week 34 flips that instruction. This is the first of a three-week finale, and the job changes from producing to packaging. Everything they need already exists. The work now is choosing what to show and making it look like it means something.

That is the reframe I open with, because students walk in expecting to build a brand-new thing for the showcase. They don't. They curate.

## What showcase-ready actually means

We put one plain standard on the board and hold everyone to it:

> Showcase-ready means someone who has never met you understands your business in two minutes and believes it is real.

Two minutes. A stranger. Believes it is real. That last part is the one students skip. A deck can be pretty and still leave a guest unsure whether any of it happened.

Three pieces get a student to that standard, and we name them on day one so the whole finale has a shape:

- **A Business Snapshot** - one page that captures the whole business at a glance.
- **A Showcase Deck** - a clean, visual walk-through of the story and the results.
- **A confident spoken presentation** - which we rehearse next week, not this one.

This week builds the first two. Keep students producing, not planning. The temptation in a finale is to talk about the showcase for three weeks. We make things instead.

## The one page that has to carry everything

The Business Snapshot is the anchor deliverable, and I like it because it is brutal about space. One page forces a choice about what actually matters.

Five things go on it:

- **Name and one-line description** - what the business is, in a sentence.
- **The problem and who has it** - the pain, and the person who feels it.
- **The solution and what makes it different** - the fix, plus the USP.
- **Traction** - real numbers, sales, customers, or tests. Proof, not adjectives.
- **The ask** - what is next, or what they want from the room.

Traction is the line students want to fudge, so we hold it hardest. A business that ran three customer interviews and made four dollars has more standing in that room than one with a beautiful logo and zero contact with a real person. Numbers you actually got beat claims you are hoping land.

The move I repeat all week: this is curation, not creation. It all already exists somewhere in their portfolio. They are pulling their strongest year of evidence forward, not inventing a business overnight.

## How we actually run the build

Monday is the stranger test. Students draft the snapshot fast, then I walk the room asking one question: could someone who has never met you read this and get your business in sixty seconds? What is missing, what is fuzzy? That question does more than any rubric.

Tuesday they finalize in a clean format - Canva, Docs, or Slides - something professional enough to print and set on the showcase table. This is also where the Week 20 brand work comes back. Same name, same colors, same wordmark, same voice across the snapshot and the deck. Consistency is what makes a small business read as legit. Different fonts on every page reads as a school project.

AI is a fair helper here, used the way we have used it all year. Hand it the snapshot text and ask it to tighten the thing so a stranger gets the business in sixty seconds and to flag anything vague. It drafts and flags. The student still decides what is true.

Thursday they build the Showcase Deck, evolving the Week 17 McCloskey deck with a full year of results behind it. A clean flow works: Cover, Problem, Solution, Market, Traction, What I Learned, What's Next. Then pairs trade feedback - one thing that is strong, one thing that is unclear, one question a showcase guest would actually ask.

## Where students get stuck

The most common trap is confusing polish with proof. A gorgeous deck with weak results loses to a plain deck with strong real numbers, every time, in front of an audience that came to see something true. We make students feel that directly in Friday's Founder Fight, pitting a pretty-but-empty deck against a plain-but-real one and letting the room argue it out.

The second trap is facts over story. A founder who walks a guest through what they noticed, tried, and learned holds a room better than one who reads a list of features. The snapshot and deck are containers for a story, not a spec sheet.

The third is the snapshot that needs explaining. If a student has to stand next to it and narrate for it to make sense, it is not done. The page has to work when they are three feet away talking to someone else.

## What good looks like by Friday

By the end of the week a student should have a printable Business Snapshot and a full showcase deck sitting in their portfolio, both in a consistent brand, both pointing at the same clear story.

They should be able to say the one-line version of their business out loud, name their single strongest piece of proof, and point to the one weak slide they still need to fix before rehearsal week. Not a perfect presentation. A packaged one.

The reframe to leave them with is the whole spirit of the finale. You are not starting over. You already did the work this year. Now you make a stranger see it in two minutes and believe it is real.
