# Validating Key Assumptions

_Every business runs on a stack of things the founder hopes are true. This week students find the shakiest ones and test them in the real world, before those guesses turn into expensive mistakes._

Ask a student how their business is going and you usually get a confident answer. People will pay this price. Customers will find me online. My supplier will deliver on time. It sounds like knowledge. Most of it is hope wearing a knowledge costume.

Every pitch is a stack of assumptions. Things the founder believes but has not actually proven. That is fine early on, you have to start somewhere. The trouble comes when you build a whole plan on a guess and never check it.

So Week 16 does one job. It teaches students to separate what they know from what they are hoping, find the guesses that would hurt most if they were wrong, and go test them before Week 17.

## An assumption is a bet you have not checked

We start by getting two words straight.

> An assumption is something you believe to be true but haven't proven yet. A fact is something you've tested and confirmed.

That gap is where businesses quietly die. Not from bad ideas, from untested ones.

Then we sort the risky assumptions into three types, so students can see what kind of bet they are actually making:

- **Desirability** - do customers actually want this?
- **Feasibility** - can I actually deliver it?
- **Viability** - will this make money?

We put one line on the board and argue about it. "People will pay $25 for my handmade candles." Is that a desirability bet, a viability bet, or both? Usually both, and students who thought they had one clean assumption find they were stacking two.

## Map first, then pick the ones that matter

The Monday activity is assumption mapping, and it is the spine of the week.

Each student lists five to seven assumptions their business depends on. Not the polished ones, the load-bearing ones. Then they rank each on two axes.

- **Importance** - how much breaks if this is wrong? (1 = low, 5 = critical)
- **Confidence** - how sure am I that it's true? (1 = just guessing, 5 = tested and confirmed)

The math does the teaching. High importance plus low confidence is the danger corner. Those are the assumptions carrying the most weight while resting on the least proof.

Students circle their top three "must test" assumptions and say one out loud to a partner. That is the shift I am watching for. They stop defending the whole business and start naming the one bet that scares them.

## A test is a sentence, not a vibe

Tuesday we build the test. The mistake students make is asking friends "would you buy this?" and calling it validation. A nice word is not data.

So we give them a three-part shape and make them fill in every blank:

- **Hypothesis** - "I believe [X] is true."
- **Test** - "I will [do this specific thing] to find out."
- **Success metric** - "I'll know it's true if [specific result]."

Here is the one we walk through together. "I believe busy parents in our neighborhood will pay $8 for cookie delivery. I will post an Instagram story with a pre-order link. I'll know it's true if 5 or more click and at least 3 pay a deposit."

Notice the money. The strongest tests put something real on the line - pre-orders, in-person demos, door-to-door asks with a payment on the table. The weak ones are surveys and social polls, where people say yes for free and never show up.

Before Wednesday I read every student's design and flag any test that is too vague to produce a real answer in a day or two. If the test cannot fail, it cannot teach.

## Run it, then decide

Wednesday is test day. Students who can run it in class do it now - make the calls, send the texts, stand up the pre-order form, demo in the hallway. Students whose test needs more time build the full plan for tonight and tomorrow: who they will reach, what they will say, what counts as a yes.

Then Thursday they come back with results, and we teach the three honest responses to any test:

- **Persevere** - the assumption held, keep going and build on it.
- **Pivot** - the assumption was wrong, change one key thing and retest.
- **Pause** - not enough data yet, run it again or run it differently.

We celebrate the failed tests hardest. A confirmed wrong assumption is not a loss, it is expensive information bought cheap. The student who learned nobody will pay $25 just saved themselves months of building the wrong thing.

## Where students get stuck

Two places, every year.

The first is confusing opinion for evidence. "My friend said they'd buy it" feels like proof and is not. We keep pushing from "would you?" toward "here's the link, want to order one right now?" Real sales beat pre-orders beat surveys beat a friend being nice.

The second is fear of a no. Some students quietly design a test that cannot fail, because a real test risks a real rejection. That is the moment to lean in. The point of this week is not to feel good, it is to know something true. A clean no on Wednesday is worth more than a hopeful maybe carried all the way to the pitch.

## What "good" looks like by Friday

By Friday you are not looking for a business that works. You are looking for a student who can tell you what they know, what they don't, and what they are willing to bet on.

Concretely, a strong student can name their riskiest assumption, point to one real data point they got from the world this week, and say which decision it led to - persevere, pivot, or pause. Even a small, messy number counts, as long as it came from outside their own head.

We close with Founder Fight and a simple judge prompt: which business has stronger proof that customers want their product? The evidence ladder settles it. Real sales over pre-orders over surveys over "my friend said."

That ladder is the habit we are really building. Next week is the Unit 2 pitch, and the Traction slide only has something on it if the testing happened here. Students who ran a real test walk in with proof. The rest walk in with hope. This week is where you decide which one you are.
