# Crafting a Compelling Value Proposition + Pricing Models

_Two questions decide whether a business survives contact with a customer. Why should someone choose you, and what should they pay? This week we make students answer both without guessing._

Ask a student what they charge and you learn a lot in about four seconds. Most of the answers sound like this: "I guessed." "I looked at what other people charge." "I don't know yet." The number came from a feeling, not a reason.

That is the honest starting point for Week 15, and it is the thing we set out to fix. By Thursday, every student should have a price they can defend and a one-sentence reason why anyone would pick them. Not a guess. A decision.

## The value proposition comes first

Price is downstream of value. So before we touch a single number, we make students answer why someone should choose them. Not their category. Them, this version, at this price.

We give them a format to fill in:

> We help [specific customer] [do or achieve or avoid something] by [your unique approach], unlike [the alternative], which [its limitation].

A worked example we use: "We help busy working parents in South Bend get fresh, affordable meal kits delivered to their door by partnering with local farms, unlike national meal kit services, which are expensive and ship produce from across the country."

Notice how much work "specific" is doing. "We help people eat better" tells you nothing. The version that names the parent, the price, the Sunday afternoon they get back - that one you can actually build a business around.

## The "so what" test

The tool that makes this stick is one question, asked over and over. A student reads their value proposition, and you ask, "So what?"

- "We help people eat better." So what?
- "We help single parents spend less than $50 a week on healthy dinners without spending Sunday cooking." So what? "More time with their kids and more money in their pocket."

If a student cannot answer "so what" in one clear sentence, the value proposition is not specific enough yet. We keep pushing until the answer is concrete and believable. This is the part facilitators should protect. It is uncomfortable, and it is where the real thinking happens.

## Four ways to price, and when each one fits

Once value is clear, we teach four pricing models in plain terms so students stop treating price as a single mysterious number.

- **Cost-plus.** Your cost per unit times your margin. It costs $12, you want 50% margin, you charge $18. Good for physical products with clear, consistent costs.
- **Value-based.** Price on what the outcome is worth to the customer. A tax preparer charges $300 because they save the customer far more than that. Good for services, expertise, and anything that saves someone time.
- **Competitive.** Price relative to the market - above it (premium), at it (competing on quality), or below it (volume). We put one warning on "below": it is very hard to raise prices later once you have trained people to expect cheap.
- **Subscription or recurring.** A regular fee for ongoing access. Good for services, communities, and content, and the reason people love it is predictable revenue.

The lesson is not "pick the right one." It is that price is a strategic choice with reasons behind it. A student who can say "my price is $X because" has already beaten most of the room.

## Price is a signal, not just a receipt

The idea I most want students to walk away with: a price tells the customer who you are before they ever buy. It signals who the product is for and what the experience will feel like. Priced too low and people quietly assume it is low quality. Priced high with nothing to back it up and they feel taken. The number is a message, and students get to decide what it says.

Wednesday we make this real with a "would you rather." Charge $5 and sell 200, or charge $20 and sell 40? Both get you to the same revenue, and that is the point. Pricing is strategy, not gut feel, and two very different businesses can hide behind the same monthly number.

## From a price to a plan

The week does not end at a price tag. Students build a simple revenue model on top of it: price times units equals revenue, then a realistic month, a goal month, and a stretch month. Fifteen dollars times twenty units is $300 a month, which is $3,600 a year. Suddenly the number on the price tag connects to a number they actually care about, and the abstract becomes a target.

By Friday, in one-on-ones, we are asking three questions: what is your price and why, what is your realistic monthly target, and what is the one thing blocking you from selling more. If a student's price is clearly unsustainable - too low to cover cost, or too high with nothing to justify it - that is the flag to catch here, while it is still cheap to fix.

## Where students get stuck

Two failure modes show up every year. The first is vagueness. Students hide behind words like "quality" and "affordable" because specific claims feel risky. The fix is the "so what" test, run relentlessly. The second is fear of charging. Plenty of students want to price at almost nothing because it feels safer and kinder. The gentle correction is that a price too low to sustain the business is not generous, it just means the business quietly dies and helps no one.

Get this week right and something clicks for students that a lot of adults never sort out. Value and price are choices you make on purpose, backed by reasons, not numbers you apologize for.
