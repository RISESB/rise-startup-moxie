# Observing Your World

_The biggest mistake early founders make is building something nobody needs. Week 3 fixes that upstream, by teaching students to go find real problems instead of guessing at them from a desk._

Most people walk through their own neighborhood every day and see nothing. Not because there is nothing there. Because they are not looking.

That is the quiet problem Week 3 solves. We make noticing a discipline students can run on purpose.

The guiding question does a lot of work: what problems are hiding in plain sight, and how do you train yourself to see them?

## Start with the problem, not the solution

Here is the reframe we put up front, because it is the whole reason this week exists.

The biggest mistake early entrepreneurs make is falling in love with an idea, building it, and finding out nobody wanted it. They started with the solution and guessed at the problem.

We teach the opposite order. Find a real problem first, out in the world, by going and looking. A classroom is a bad place to guess what people need. The street is a good place to find out.

So Week 3 is not about ideas at all. It is about seeing. The idea can wait.

## Walk, Talk, Show Up

The core method has three moves, named plainly so students can actually run them.

- **Walk** - move through the world with your eyes open, hunting for three things: **friction** (something harder than it should be), **gaps** (something missing or underserved), and **patterns** (something you see more than once, in more than one place).
- **Talk** - have natural, ad-hoc conversations about what you are noticing. Not a survey, not a pitch. The goal is to understand someone's experience, not to sell them yours.
- **Show Up** - put yourself in the spaces where the people you want to help already are. Not to sell. To understand. And you show up give-first, contributing before you ask for anything.

A Walk does not have to be a literal walk. It can be scrolling a community Facebook group, reading Yelp reviews, sitting in a waiting room. Anywhere people run into problems counts.

The Talk piece is where students slip, so we make the difference concrete. Good Talk sounds like "I noticed this - does that happen to you a lot?" Bad Talk sounds like "I have this idea for an app, would you buy it?"

One set gets you what you want to hear. The other gets you what you need to know. That gap is the whole lesson.

Show Up is the one students underrate. I tell them it widens the number of places you can get lucky. The more rooms you are in, the better your odds of finding the real problem worth solving.

## The 5 Whys: dig past the surface

Most first observations are shallow. "The lines are long." "The app is slow." True, but not useful yet. The 5 Whys gets underneath.

It is what it sounds like. Start with an observation, ask why, take the answer, ask why again. Do it about five times.

We run one on the board together so they feel it:

> The cafeteria always has long lines. Why? Only two registers open at peak. Why? Only two staff scheduled. Why? The budget only covers two for that shift. Why? Staffing was never prioritized in the budget. Why? Nobody has data on how much student time the lines actually waste.

We started at "long lines" and landed at "a data problem." Completely different problems, and they lead to completely different solutions.

That is the point I want students to leave with. The 5 Whys tells you where the real opportunity is, which is almost never where you first looked.

When groups practice this on Tuesday, the facilitator's only job is to push. When a group stops at an easy answer, that is usually why number two. "Keep going. What causes that?"

## The Discovery Tracker: their field notebook

None of this sticks if it lives in their heads, so every observation gets logged. The Discovery Tracker carries the week, and it has five columns:

- **Observation** - what did you see or hear?
- **Where / When** - context matters.
- **Who is affected** - be specific, not "people."
- **5 Whys** - dig to the root.
- **Possible opportunity** - what could this become?

Students start filling it in Monday using their own warm-up observations, and they keep adding all week. The target is at least three complete entries, each with a full 5 Whys, by Friday.

Wednesday takes the whole thing outside on the speaker or tour. Their job there is not just to listen. It is to observe like an entrepreneur, spotting gaps and friction in a real environment and logging them on the spot.

## Where students get stuck

Two places, every year.

They stop the 5 Whys too early, landing on the first tidy answer and calling it the root cause. That is why the facilitator keeps pushing groups past why number two.

And they generalize the "who." "Students hate long lines" is not an observation you can act on. "Freshmen with fourth-period lunch miss the salad bar because it is empty by the time they get there" is. Specific beats broad every time, and the tracker's columns force it.

Friday we tie it back to money with Profit or Broke, running quick business scenarios so students start feeling the numbers behind the problems they have been watching. A problem gets more interesting once you can see the dollars moving through it.

## What good looks like by Friday

You are not looking for a business idea this week. You are looking for a student who sees differently than Monday.

By Friday a strong student can point to at least one problem worth exploring, name specifically who has it, and tell you whether they have talked to anyone who lives it. Their tracker has three real entries dug to the root, not three shallow gripes.

We close with the reflection that matters most: what would you need to know to be confident this is a real problem worth solving? That question is the whole job of an entrepreneur, and it is the bridge into Week 4, where we take the strongest problem and start building the simplest possible fix.

Be a detective this week. The problems were always there. Now you are looking.
