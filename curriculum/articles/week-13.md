# Choosing the Right Channels + Competitive Research + SWOT Analysis

_You can build a great product, price it right, and target the perfect customer, and still fail if you show up in the wrong place. Week 13 is about reaching your customer where they already are, and knowing exactly why they would pick you over everyone else trying to do the same._

Most students think the hard part is the product. Get the thing good enough and customers will find it. That is the myth this week takes apart.

You can build a great product, price it perfectly, and target the right customer, and still fail if you are trying to reach them in the wrong place. A product sitting alone in an empty room does not sell itself.

So Week 13 does two jobs at once. Students learn how to reach their customer where they already are, and they learn to look hard at everyone else trying to reach that same customer. Channels and competition, taught together, because they answer the same question: how do you get in front of someone, and why should they choose you once you do.

## The channel is how you reach your customer

We put it plainly on Monday. The channel is how you reach your customer. The right channel is where they already are, already trust information, and are already making decisions.

There are two families of channels, and we teach both so students stop defaulting to "post on Instagram and hope."

- **Direct channels** - in person (events, markets, door-to-door, community spaces), direct outreach (DMs, texts, calls, emails), and your own social media.
- **Indirect channels** - referrals (existing customers tell others), partnerships (another business sends you customers), marketplaces (Etsy, eBay, Facebook Marketplace), and press and content (stories, podcasts, blogs).

Then the line that reframes the whole thing:

> The best channel is the one your specific customer already uses and trusts. Go back to your persona. Where does your customer spend time?

The worst move is trying every channel and being mediocre everywhere. One bright spotlight beats ten scattered, dim ones. We push students to pick one or two and be excellent there first.

## You always have competition

The second reframe lands harder, because students want to believe they are the only solution. They are not.

Even if there is no direct competitor, your customer has a workaround, and that workaround is your real competition. The person solving the problem with a clumsy DIY fix is choosing something other than you right now. That choice is what you are up against.

So we teach a simple research method students can actually run:

- **Google the problem** from your customer's perspective. What comes up?
- **Search their watering holes.** What do people recommend to each other?
- **Read the reviews.** Review sites, Reddit, local Facebook groups. Who are people already paying?
- **Ask AI.** "What are the existing solutions for [my problem]? What do customers complain about most?"

The complaints are the gold. What competitors do badly is the gap you might be able to walk into.

## How we actually run it: channel mapping and SWOT

Monday is the lecture. Tuesday is where it becomes real work, and this is the part I care about most.

First, channel mapping. Students list every possible channel, then score each one on two things: how well it reaches their persona, and how feasible it is right now. Top three get circled. I walk the room asking one question over and over: "Your persona spends time at this place. Is that channel on your list?" That question closes the gap between where the customer actually is and where the student wishes they were.

Then competitive research. Students study 2 to 3 competitors and, for each, write down the name, the offer and price, the target customer, what they do well (positive reviews), what they do badly (negative reviews), and how their own business compares.

That research feeds straight into a SWOT analysis, and we teach the one distinction that keeps SWOT from turning to mush:

- **Strengths** are internal. What do you have that competitors don't? Your Bird-in-Hand from earlier in the year is a clue.
- **Weaknesses** are internal. What do your discovery interviews say you still don't have?
- **Opportunities** are external. What gap in the competitive landscape did you just find?
- **Threats** are external. What could kill this? A market shift, a competitor copying you, a cost change.

Strengths and Weaknesses live inside your business. Opportunities and Threats live out in the market. Students mix these up constantly, so we name it clearly and keep pointing back to it.

The payoff is one instruction: circle your biggest Strength and your biggest Opportunity. That intersection is your competitive advantage. It is the specific thing that makes a customer choose you over their current workaround.

## Where students get stuck

The most common stall is the vague competitive advantage. "We care more." "Better quality." That is not an advantage, it is a hope. On Friday I have students say their advantage in one sentence and I note which ones are specific and which are fuzzy, because the fuzzy ones are the ones with more work to do.

The second stall is the everywhere problem. A student wants to be on every channel because picking two feels like giving something up. Founder Fight is built to press on exactly this. We run a business with one strong channel against one that tries to be everywhere, and a business with no competitors against one with ten established ones. The debrief asks what competition actually signals about a market. Usually it signals that people are already paying, which is good news, not bad.

The third stall is treating "no competitors" as a win. It almost never is. Either the workaround is the competition, or nobody wants the thing.

## What good looks like by Friday

By the end of the week, a student should be able to name their top three channels and one concrete action for each. Not a wish list. Three real places their customer already is, with a first move for each one.

They should have a completed SWOT that keeps internal and external straight, built from actual competitor research instead of guesses.

And they should be able to finish this sentence with something specific: "A customer chooses me over their current workaround because ______."

We close the way we close every week, with each student saying one clear thing out loud. Here it is their competitive advantage, in a single sentence, specific enough that the person next to them believes it. If it still sounds like everyone else, that is the signal to keep sharpening. Next week we build the sales process that turns all of this into an actual sale.
