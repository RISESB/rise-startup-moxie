# Financial Statements + Budgeting

_Most students think financial statements are a math test they will fail. This week they learn to read the health of a business at a glance, then plan their money before they spend it._

Say the words "financial statements" to a room of teenagers and you can watch the shoulders drop. They hear a math class they already decided they are bad at. Spreadsheets, decimals, a wrong answer waiting to embarrass them.

That reaction is the thing to fix first. Reading a business is not a math test. It is reading three short reports that answer three plain questions, and every serious business on earth uses the same three, from a lemonade stand to Apple.

So Week 28 does two jobs. Students learn to read the health of a business at a glance, and they learn to plan their money on purpose instead of spending reactively and getting surprised.

## Three statements, three questions

We open by putting the three reports on the board and refusing to make them complicated. Each one answers one question.

- **Income Statement (P&L)** - "Did I make money over a period of time?" Revenue minus costs equals profit. This is the one they will use most.
- **Balance Sheet** - "What do I own and what do I owe, right now?" Assets equal liabilities plus equity. A snapshot in time.
- **Cash Flow Statement** - "Where did my cash actually move?" They already built a version of this last week.

The line I want them to leave with is the whole lecture in one breath:

> Income statement = did I make money. Balance sheet = what am I worth. Cash flow = do I have money right now. Three different questions, three statements.

Once that clicks, the fear usually drops. It is not a wall of numbers. It is three questions a founder asks about their own business.

## Reading a P&L without flinching

The income statement is where we spend the real time, because it is the one they will actually run every month.

We walk a simple one down the board, line by line. Revenue $1,000. Cost of goods sold, the variable cost of making the thing, minus $350. That leaves gross profit of $650. Then fixed and operating costs, minus $400. What is left is net profit, $250.

Gross profit is what you have after the cost of making the product. Net profit is what you have after everything. When someone says "the bottom line," this is literally the bottom line, the last number on the page.

Then we flip from watching to doing. In Read and React, students work through two or three sample P&Ls I hand them and answer three questions about each: Is this business healthy? What is the biggest cost? What would you fix first?

That last question is the one that turns a reader into an operator. Anyone can add the numbers. Deciding what to fix is the skill.

## A budget is a plan, not a report card

Budgeting is the second half of the week, and it deserves its own reframe. Students tend to think a budget is something you look at after the money is gone, a report card on how you did.

A budget is the opposite. It is a plan for your money before you spend it. Businesses without one spend reactively and get surprised. A budget lets you decide on purpose.

The version we teach is one page. Expected revenue at the top. Planned spending by category underneath, meaning materials, marketing, fees, and savings or reinvestment. Then what is left.

The phrase we repeat is "give every dollar a job before the month starts." And one rule sits above all of it: if it is not in the budget, you pause before spending.

On Tuesday they build both for their own business, using real sales numbers if they have them and their realistic projection from Week 26 if they do not.

## Where students get stuck

The first snag is confusing gross and net. A student sees the bigger gross number and thinks the business is healthier than it is. The fix is to keep pointing at the bottom line and asking "after everything, what is actually left?"

The second is a budget that is a wish, not a plan. Numbers that look tidy but do not match what the business actually spends. In 1-on-1s I have them walk me through it out loud: "What is your net profit, and what does your budget say you can reinvest?" Saying it out loud is where the soft spots show up.

The third snag is the one worth protecting. A budget that never touches a real decision is just a worksheet. So the point of the week is not the document, it is the choice it lets you make.

That is why Founder Fight this week is a money-management edition. Reinvest every dollar into growth versus keep a cash cushion. Track every number versus "just knowing" how you are doing. Spend big on marketing versus spend big on product quality. There is no single right answer, and that is the lesson. What matters is deciding on purpose, with the numbers in front of you.

## What good looks like by Friday

By Friday I am not looking for a finance whiz. I am looking for a student who can pull up their own income statement, point to their net profit, and tell me what their budget says they can reinvest this month.

The proof is a real decision. Something like "based on my budget, here is what I can afford to spend on growth this month, and here is what I will spend it on." A number that came from a plan, not a gut feeling.

We close with reflection, one sentence each. What does your income statement tell you about your business? What is one spending decision your budget helped you make? Which of the three statements matters most to you right now, and why?

The reframe to send them home with is small and durable. You are not bad at this. You just needed the three questions and a plan. Read the health, then decide on purpose.
