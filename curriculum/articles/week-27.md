# Cash Flow + Break-Even Analysis

_Profit tells you the score at the end of the game. Cash flow tells you whether you can pay for the next play. This week students learn the difference, and find the exact number of sales that stops the bleeding._

Most people think a business dies because it is not profitable. That is not usually what kills it.

More businesses die from running out of cash than from being unprofitable. You can be profitable on paper and still go broke, because the money you earned has not arrived yet and the bill on your desk is due today.

That gap between "earned" and "in hand" is the whole subject this week. The guiding question we put in front of students is blunt: when does the money actually arrive, and how many sales until you stop losing money?

## Profit is the score, cash is your pocket

We start by splitting two words students tend to treat as the same thing.

Profit is the final score at the end of the game. Cash flow is whether you have money in your pocket right now. Both matter, but only one of them pays for materials this week.

The example we use lands fast. You land a $1,000 order, which is real profit. But you have to spend $400 on materials now, and the customer pays you in 30 days. For those 30 days you are $400 in the hole. If you cannot cover it, the deal that should have made you money sinks you instead.

That is the trap I want students to feel before they feel it in real life. A good deal on paper can still break you if the timing is wrong.

## Money in, money out, and the gap between them

Once the two words are separate, cash flow gets simple. It tracks two things over time: when money actually comes in, and when it actually goes out. The gap between them is where businesses get in trouble.

We give students three terms to hold onto:

- **Positive cash flow** - more coming in than going out this period.
- **Negative cash flow** - more going out than coming in. Fine for a short stretch, deadly if it runs long.
- **Runway** - how long you can survive at your current burn rate before the cash hits zero.

On Tuesday they build it themselves. Each student makes a simple 3-month cash flow view: starting cash, money in by when it actually arrives, money out by when it is actually due, and ending cash each month.

Then we hunt for the month that goes negative and sit with it. "What would you do to stay alive that month - delay a cost, collect faster, sell more up front?" That question is the point of the whole build. A cash flow view that never gets stress-tested is just a spreadsheet.

## Break-even: the magic number

The second half of the week gives students one number that changes how they set goals.

> Break-even units = Fixed costs ÷ Contribution margin per unit.

Break-even is how many sales you need just to cover your costs. Below it you lose money. Above it you make money. That is the magic number.

The candle example makes it concrete. Fixed costs are $260 a month, the booth fee plus a supplies subscription. Each candle contributes $13 after its own costs. So $260 ÷ $13 = 20 candles. You have to sell 20 candles a month just to break even, and candle 21 is your first real profit.

Knowing that changes the goal from a vibe to a target. The monthly number is no longer "sell a lot." It is 20, then everything above it.

Students run this on their own business, using the contribution margin they built back in Week 26 and their own fixed costs. Then they answer the harder version: how many days or weeks until I hit break-even at my current sales pace, and what is the fastest realistic way to get there sooner? AI is fair game here as a thinking partner - hand it your fixed costs and margin and ask for three ways to lower the number.

## Where students get stuck

Two spots trip almost everyone up, and both are worth naming out loud.

The first is confusing profit with cash. A student sees a big order and relaxes, missing that they cannot cover the materials that make the order possible. Walk them back to the $1,000 trap every time.

The second is treating break-even as trivia instead of a decision. Finding the number is easy. The work is comparing it to what they are selling now and being honest about the gap. When a student says "my break-even is 20 and I am selling 6," that is not a failure, that is the most useful sentence they will say all week.

Friday is where it gets real with our Profit or Broke round, cash-timing edition. You have $50 cash. You get a $300 order but must spend $120 on materials now, and the customer pays in three weeks. Profit or broke, right now? Profitable order, cash-broke today. Then the follow-up that matters: how do you make this deal survivable?

## What good looks like by Friday

By the end of the week you are not looking for a perfect model. You are looking for a student who can hold two numbers and one habit.

They can say, in their own words, the difference between profit and cash flow. They can name their break-even point as a specific number of sales, not a guess. And they can point to one concrete move to get cash in faster or hit break-even sooner.

We close the way we always do, with each student saying one number out loud: how many sales to break even. One number, around the room, no hiding.

That number is the quiet shift this week is after. A student who walks in thinking money is a feeling and walks out able to say "I need 20 sales a month and I am at 6" is thinking like an operator. Everything downstream, the budgeting and the statements coming next week, sits on top of that.
