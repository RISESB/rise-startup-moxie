# Legal Structures + Operations

_Two boring words separate a hobby from a real business: legal and operations. This week we make both plain enough that a student can act on them, without pretending to be lawyers._

Nobody signs up for a startup class hoping to talk about legal structures. It sounds like paperwork, and paperwork is the opposite of building something.

But here is the thing students miss. The two least exciting parts of running a business, being set up right and running on systems, are the two that quietly decide whether it survives a good week. Get them right and everything else gets easier.

So Week 30 does two jobs. We give students a map of how businesses get made official, and we teach them why a business that lives only in your head breaks the moment you get busy. Neither piece is legal advice. It is a map so they know what to ask.

## The guiding question

We put one question on the board and let it run all week:

> How do you make your business official, keep it legal, and run it so it doesn't depend on you remembering everything?

That last clause is the one that lands. Most students think a business is a thing you have. We want them to see it as a thing you run, and something you run has to work when you are not there.

## The four structures, in plain terms

The legal piece is short on purpose. Four options go on the board, smallest to biggest:

- **Sole proprietorship** - you ARE the business. Cheapest and easiest to start. The catch is there is no separation, so you are personally on the hook for any debts.
- **LLC** - a limited liability company separates you from the business, so your personal stuff is protected if things go wrong. The most common choice for small businesses.
- **Partnership** - two or more owners share it. Simple to start, but you need a clear written agreement so a good friendship does not turn into a bad fight.
- **Corporation** - a C-corp or S-corp, the most complex, built for raising big investment and scaling. Overkill for almost everyone starting out.

Then we name the one word that carries the whole lesson: liability. Limited liability means if the business owes money or gets sued, they generally cannot come after your personal savings. That protection is the entire reason the LLC is so popular.

The takeaway we want them to leave with is not "file an LLC." It is a sequence. Most of you start as a sole prop because it is free and fast, then move to an LLC once you have real revenue and real risk worth protecting.

## Staying legal without drowning in it

Depending on the business, staying legal can mean a business name registration, a license or permit, a way to handle sales tax if you sell products, and basic records of income and expenses.

The move here is to fight the panic, not feed it. You do not need all of this on day one. You just need to know which ones apply to you so nothing surprises you later.

This is a good place to put AI to work. Students ask it, "I run a [type] business in [state], what licenses or permits typically apply?" Then, and this is the part we repeat, they verify anything that matters before acting on it. AI is a fast way to know what to ask, not a lawyer.

## Systems beat memory

Operations sounds abstract until you define it plainly. Operations is just how the work actually gets done, repeatably. When everything lives in your head, the business breaks the moment you are busy, sick, or growing.

The tool we hand them is the SOP, a Standard Operating Procedure. It is a simple step-by-step for one task that anyone could follow. How I fulfill an order. How I onboard a customer. Boring, and it is exactly what lets a business grow past you.

The way we make this real is a question we call the 50-orders test. If a good problem hit tomorrow and 50 orders came in at once, could someone help you using only your notes? SOPs turn that from a no into a yes.

On Tuesday students write their first real one. They pick their most important repeatable task, write it step by step, then swap with a partner. The test is blunt: could you actually do this using only these steps, and where would you get stuck? A gap the partner finds is worth more than a page that looks tidy.

## Where students get stuck

Two places, every year.

The first is treating the legal decision as permanent. A sole prop is not a life sentence, and a student who freezes trying to pick the "right" structure has missed the point. The answer is a structure for now and a trigger for later, written in one sentence: I would operate as a sole prop now, and switch to an LLC when I have steady revenue and real risk.

The second is writing an SOP that only they could follow. It reads fine to the author, who already knows the missing steps. That is why the partner swap matters. The gaps someone else hits are the whole reason to write it down.

## What good looks like by Friday

By Friday you are not looking for a filed LLC or a perfect operations manual. You are looking for two clear artifacts and a shift in how a student sees their own business.

A structure decision they can say out loud, with a trigger for when it changes. One legal or permit item they know to look into. A first SOP a stranger could actually run. And a short list of the top three systems the business still needs next.

We close with Profit or Broke scenarios that make the stakes concrete. Skip a fifty-dollar permit, get shut down at a market, lose a three-hundred-dollar day. Spend three hours writing SOPs, then handle double the volume next month because a friend could step in using them. The point students feel is that the boring work has a number attached.

Next week we move to the numbers themselves. But a business that is set up right and runs on systems instead of memory can finally grow beyond the person who started it. That is the quiet payoff of the least exciting week of the year.
