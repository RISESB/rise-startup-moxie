# Business Model Canvas + MVPs + May-B-P

_Last week students found problems. This week they take the first swing at solving one - not by building the dream business, but by building the smallest test that proves anyone cares._

Ask most people how a business starts and they describe the finish line. The polished app, the logo, the website, the launch. All the stuff that comes after somebody has already proven the thing works.

That order is backwards, and it is where new founders lose the most time. They build for months, then go looking for a customer, and find out too late that nobody wanted it.

Week 4 flips the order. The question we put in front of students is simple: what is the simplest version of your business you could build right now to test if the problem is real?

Three tools answer that question, and we teach them in one Monday lecture: the Business Model Canvas, the MVP, and the May-B-P.

## The canvas is a map, not a plan

We start with the Business Model Canvas because it makes an idea visible. It is a one-page map of how a business works, created by Alexander Osterwalder, used by startups and Fortune 500 companies alike.

Nine boxes, each answering one question about the business:

- **Customer Segments** - who are you building this for?
- **Value Proposition** - what problem do you solve, and why is your solution better?
- **Channels** - how do you reach your customers?
- **Customer Relationships** - how do you get, keep, and grow customers?
- **Revenue Streams** - how do you make money?
- **Key Resources** - what do you need to deliver the value?
- **Key Activities** - what do you actually do every day?
- **Key Partners** - who do you need to work with?
- **Cost Structure** - what does it cost to run this?

We fill one in out loud as a class, usually for a familiar local business like a food truck or a barbershop, students calling out answers box by box.

Here is the line I want them to leave with: filling this out raises more questions than it answers. That is the point. The canvas is a hypothesis, not a plan. It shows you what you are guessing about so you know what to go test.

## MVP, then May-B-P

Next comes the MVP, short for Minimum Viable Product. It is the simplest version of your product that delivers enough value for someone to actually use it and give you feedback.

The word that carries the weight is *viable*. It has to work. Not perfect, not finished, but it has to deliver the core value.

The example that lands every time is Dropbox. Their first MVP was a three-minute demo video. They did not build the product first - they filmed what it would do and put it online, and 75,000 people signed up overnight. That told them the market was real before they wrote a line of code.

Then we introduce the version we actually use, a Startup Moxie idea rather than a textbook one: the May-B-P, the Maybe-a-Product. The definition we write on the board:

> A May-B-P is a business you can build in 2 weeks that tests whether the market is interested in solving the problem - not whether this is the scalable solution.

A May-B-P is not the dream business. It is the first experiment. We show students the Professor Burger clip and ask what he did *not* do when he started. He did not build a restaurant. He did not wait for the perfect setup. He started with what he had and tested the idea.

## Now vs. Later strips it to the core

Knowing you should start small and actually starting small are two different things. The bridge is a framework we run on Tuesday called Now vs. Later.

Students brainstorm every step they think they need to launch - everything, written fast, no filtering. Then they sort the list into two columns:

- **Now** - anything you need to test whether someone will pay for your solution.
- **Later** - everything else. Logos, websites, business cards, legal setup, scale plans.

Take a meal prep service. Now: cook one meal, find one customer, sell it. Later: build the website, design the menu, get the food handler's permit.

When the sorting is done, I ask the same question every time: what is actually left in your Now column? That short list is your May-B-P. The framework does the reframe for them. Most of what felt urgent turns out to be Later in disguise.

From there they draft the concept on four questions: what is it, who is the first customer, what does success look like in two weeks, and what is the one thing you need to do first.

## Where students get stuck

Two places, and I watch for both.

The first is the blank box. A student stares at Customer Segments and writes nothing because they are not sure. The fix I repeat all week: a guess is better than a blank, because a guess forces you to think. We can fix a wrong answer. We cannot fix nothing.

The second is the vague customer. "Teenagers" is not a customer. "High school athletes in South Bend who need affordable sports gear" is. In the 1-on-1s I keep asking until it gets real: do you know your first customer by name? If they cannot name a person, the May-B-P is still an idea, not a test.

The deeper trap under both is waiting. Waiting for the app, the logo, the right moment. The whole week is built to break that habit and push toward the one thing that matters: go find out if anyone cares.

## What good looks like by Friday

By Friday you are not looking for a finished business. You are looking for a student who can hold up a canvas, point to the box they are least sure about, and say the one real-world action their May-B-P needs this week - and when they are going to do it.

We close with reflection, and my favorite question is the one that exposes the waiting: what are you still holding out for that you don't actually need yet?

A student who can answer that, and who has one small thing they can do this weekend to test their idea, is exactly where they should be. Everything after this - outreach, pricing, the pitch - sits on top of a test they were willing to run before they felt ready.
