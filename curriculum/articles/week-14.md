# Sales Strategy

_Ask a teenager what they think of sales and you get "pushy, fake, scary." Week 14 takes that word apart and hands students a process they can actually design._

Say the word "sales" in a room of high schoolers and watch their faces. Pushy. Uncomfortable. Fake. Scary. We run this as the Monday warm-up on purpose, one word around the room, all of it up on the board where everyone can see it.

Then we say the quiet part out loud. That list is not describing sales. It is describing bad sales, the kind everyone has been on the receiving end of. And it is the reason most young founders avoid the one thing their business cannot live without.

So Week 14 does one job. It reframes selling from something you do *to* people into something you do *for* them, and it hands students a map they can draw, walk, and fix.

## Selling is helping, not convincing

Here is the sentence we put on the board and sit with:

> Selling is not convincing people to buy something they don't want. It is helping people who have a problem find the solution they are already looking for.

That reframe only works because of the weeks that came before it. By now students have done real customer discovery. The problem is real, and people want it solved. Sales is just the mechanism that connects them to the solution.

If you skip the reframe, students think their job is to talk someone into something. That version of sales feels gross, so they never do it. The version where you help a person who already has the problem, that one they can live with. Most of the fear drains out of the room right here.

## The 5-stage funnel

Once selling feels survivable, we make it a process. We draw a funnel on the board, wide at the top and narrow at the bottom, strangers going in and customers coming out.

- **Awareness** - they know you exist.
- **Interest** - they are curious enough to learn more.
- **Consideration** - they are comparing you to the alternatives.
- **Decision** - they decide to buy. Something has to remove the last obstacle.
- **Loyalty** - they come back, and they refer others.

The point of drawing it is not the vocabulary. It is that a funnel is something you can *design*, stage by stage, instead of hoping people show up ready to pay.

Then we name the trap. Most founders pour everything into Stage 1, the posting and the marketing, and set up nothing for Stages 3 and 4. People get interested, hit a wall where there is no clear next step, and quietly fall out. Mind the gap between Consideration and Decision. That is where the money leaks.

## Lead, prospect, customer

Students tend to lump everyone who is not yet a customer into one blurry pile. So we split the pile into three words that each mean something.

- **Lead** - someone who fits your persona and has the problem.
- **Prospect** - a lead who has expressed interest.
- **Customer** - a prospect who bought.

The reason this matters is that it turns a vague feeling ("sales is slow") into a number you can look at. Track your conversion rate at each step. If a hundred leads become two prospects, your problem is early. If ten prospects become zero customers, your problem is the close. You cannot fix what you have not named.

## Door openers

The last piece of Monday is my favorite, because it is the one students never think of on their own. In every market there are people who have access to a lot of others and who those others already trust. We call them door openers.

One relationship with a door opener can open ten customer conversations. It is the difference between knocking on a hundred doors and having someone walk you through the front hall.

- **Selling to parents** - the PTA president, a pediatrician, a school counselor.
- **Selling to small businesses** - the chamber of commerce director, or a bookkeeper who already serves twenty businesses.
- **Selling to athletes** - the head coach, the athletic director, the team trainer.

The 1-on-1 question all week is simple: who are the three door openers in your market, and are they on your outreach list yet?

## Where students get stuck

Two places, reliably.

The first is Stage 3 to Stage 4. When you walk the room during the funnel draft, most students can tell you how someone becomes aware and interested, then go blank on what happens when a person is interested but has not bought. The prompt that unsticks them is blunt: "What do you actually do next? What is the message you send?"

The second is outreach. On Tuesday students draft a three-message sequence with AI, and the drafts come back sounding like a press release. Message 1 names the problem with no ask. Message 2 shares one piece of value and a soft ask to connect. Message 3 makes the direct ask, a ten-minute call. AI writes the skeleton fast. The job that is theirs, and the one they want to skip, is editing it until it sounds like a human they would actually answer.

## What good looks like by Friday

By Friday you are not looking for a closed deal. You are looking for a student who can walk you through their own funnel and point to where they are losing people.

They should have a funnel drawn, a three-message sequence that reads like a person wrote it, and at least one real door opener named and added to a list. In the week reflection they should be able to say which stage is strongest, which one needs the most work, and one person they are going to reach out to this week.

The shift I am after is the one from Monday morning. A student who walked in flinching at the word "sales" and walks out treating it as a process they can design, measure, and improve. That is the whole week. The deal comes later.
