# Effectuation + Starting with What You Have + Team Building

_Most people think entrepreneurs start with a plan and then go find the resources. Expert founders do the opposite. Week 9 teaches students to start with what they already have, and to find the two or three people who round them out._

There is a story students carry into this class without knowing it. The founder has a big vision, writes the plan, raises the money, hires the team, and executes. Plan first, resources second.

That story is not just incomplete. It is close to how most businesses actually fail.

Researchers who studied expert entrepreneurs, people who built and sold multiple companies, found they think in almost the reverse order. They start with what is already in their hands and figure out what is possible. That logic has a name, and Week 9 is where we hand it to students.

## Two ways to start: causation and effectuation

We put the two logics side by side on the board because the contrast does the teaching.

Causation starts with a goal and then hunts for the resources to reach it. It works fine when the future is predictable, and most of a student's first business is not.

Effectuation flips it. You start with your means and ask what you can build right now. The framework comes from researcher Saras Sarasvathy, who named the four principles expert founders actually run on:

- **Bird-in-Hand** - start with your means. Who are you, what do you know, who do you know?
- **Affordable Loss** - don't ask what the upside is. Ask what is the most you are willing to lose, and only risk that.
- **Crazy Quilt** - build partnerships. Every stakeholder you bring in adds their means and their constraints, and the business shifts with them.
- **Lemonade** - treat surprises as data, not failure. An unexpected result is information, not a verdict.

I tell students these are not motivational phrases. They are operating principles, and most students have been using a few by instinct already. Now they have names for them, which makes them repeatable.

## Bird-in-Hand is where a teenager actually starts

Of the four, the one that matters most for where students are right now is Bird-in-Hand. It is three plain questions.

> Who am I? What do I know? Who do I know?

Who am I is values and strengths. What do I know is skills and the things you have real reps in. Who do I know is your network, the people and access you already have.

Here is the point I want to land: the best early momentum shows up where the founder's Bird-in-Hand matches the problem they are solving.

The example we use is a student who grew up helping her mom run a small cleaning business, speaks Spanish fluently, and is connected through family to the Latino small business community in South Bend. For a business serving that market, her means and the problem line up. She does not build that from scratch, and that head start is worth naming out loud.

## You cannot build it alone, and that is not a weakness

The other half of the week is team. I say the honest version early: you cannot build a serious business by yourself. Even solo founders have a team - advisors, mentors, part-time help, contractors. So the question is never whether you need people. It is what kind of team you need, and what they bring that you do not already have.

A good team has complementary strengths, not copies of the founder. We give students three roles that show up in almost every early-stage company:

- **The Builder** - the one who can make the product or deliver the service.
- **The Seller** - the one who finds customers and closes them.
- **The Operator** - the one who keeps the thing running, tracks the money, and executes week after week.

Most students are naturally one of these. The work is knowing which one you are, and knowing where you will find the other two.

## How the week actually runs

Monday is the lecture. Tuesday is where it gets concrete, and Tuesday is the heart of the week.

Students fill in a Bird-in-Hand audit, all three columns, then hold it against their May-B-P and mark where their means align with the problem and where they do not. Walk the room. When a student's Bird-in-Hand fits their idea, point it out by name. When it does not, ask the better question: is there a version of this idea that starts closer to what you already have?

Then they build a simple team map: list the three roles, rate themselves strong fit, partial fit, or not my strength on each, and name a real person who could fill the gaps. The Crazy Quilt activity makes it live: each student writes a one-paragraph pitch for why someone should partner with them, and a partner reads it and answers one question - does this make me want to join?

We close with an Affordable Loss check: the most money, time, and public risk you will spend before you know it works. That is not pessimism. It is the thing that keeps you in the game long enough to learn something.

## Where students get stuck

Two places, reliably.

The first is a student whose idea does not match anything they have. They picked something that sounded impressive and the audit shows no means behind it. Do not talk them out of the dream. Ask for the nearer version, the one that starts on ground they already stand on.

The second is a room full of the same type. Friday's warm-up counts Builders, Sellers, and Operators out loud, and it is usually lopsided. That is the teachable moment: if you form teams in here, be intentional about balance, because three Builders and no Seller is not a team, it is a group project waiting to stall.

## What good looks like by Friday

By the end of the week a student should be able to do three things without notes. Explain effectuation in their own words and say how it differs from plan-first thinking. Point to where their Bird-in-Hand lines up with their May-B-P, and where it does not yet. And name one real person they could bring in to fill a gap they cannot fill themselves.

That is the shift. Monday they think a business starts with a plan and a pile of money. Friday they know it starts with what is already in their hands, and the few people they trust to build beside them.

Next week we get specific about who the customer actually is. This week we made sure they know what they bring to the table.
