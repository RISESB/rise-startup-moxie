# MVP Building + Testing with Early Users + Vibe Coding

_This is the week the idea stops living on paper. Students build the simplest real version of their business and put it in front of one actual person, because their confusion is the data you have been waiting for._

For eleven weeks students have been planning. Noticing problems, talking to people, sketching an offer. All of that is upstream work, and it matters. But planning has a way of feeling like progress when it isn't.

Week 12 crosses the most important line of the year. We stop planning and start building something real enough that a person outside the class can react to it.

The guiding question is small on purpose: what is the simplest version of your idea you can put in front of a real person this week, and what will you learn when you do?

Notice the goal is not perfect. The goal is real.

## What an MVP actually is

We put the term on the board and strip it down. A minimum viable product is the simplest version of your idea that a real person can react to. That is the whole definition.

What throws students is what an MVP is *not*. It is not a full website, a polished logo, a business entity, or a finished product. Every one of those feels like the work, and every one is a way to stay busy without ever showing anyone anything.

The shape of the MVP depends on the business, so we map it by type:

- **Service business** - one delivery of the service to one real customer, done by hand.
- **Product business** - a handmade or manually produced version. No factory, no inventory.
- **Digital or app** - a mockup, a landing page, or a version you deliver manually that the app will eventually automate.
- **Content or media** - one piece of content put in front of a real audience to measure engagement.

The through-line is that the student makes it themselves, this week, small. A service delivered once teaches you more than a business plan for a service delivered a thousand times.

## Testing means watching what they do

Here is the part students get wrong, and it is worth slowing down for. Testing an MVP is not asking people what they think. It is watching what they do.

> Testing your MVP is not asking people what they think. It is watching what they do.

Ask a friend if your idea is good and they will say yes, because they like you. Watch a stranger try to use it and you learn where it actually breaks. We frame a good early user test as getting three things right:

- **The right user** - someone who matches your persona, not your friends and not your mom.
- **The right context** - let them experience it as naturally as possible, without you explaining everything first.
- **The right questions** - asked after, not during: What did you expect that didn't happen? What confused you? What would make you come back?

The hardest part of the whole exercise is shutting up. When someone gets confused, the instinct is to jump in and explain. Do not. Their confusion is the data. The moment you explain it away, you have thrown out the most useful thing the test was going to give you.

## Vibe coding, for the students who need it

For students building a digital product, we open an option this week. We call it vibe coding, and it means building with AI using no-code or low-code tools, no programming experience required. Tools like Bolt.new, Glide, Carrd, and Cursor.

It is a track, not a requirement. On Tuesday the room splits. Digital builders open a tool and start prompting. Physical product and service builders skip it entirely and go make their first batch or deliver their service to a real customer.

The skill in vibe coding is not typing code. It is directing. You describe what the app should do, who uses it, what the main screen looks like, then you run the prompt, look at what came back, and tell the AI what to fix. You are managing the build like a project manager, not writing it like an engineer.

Same lesson as the rest of the year. AI gives you a fast draft. You still have to make it true and make it yours.

## Where students get stuck

Two places, every time.

The first is building forever and never shipping. A student will polish the thing for days because a real user hasn't seen it yet, and an unseen product cannot disappoint anyone. So as we walk the room we ask one question over and over: when is your first user experiencing this, and do you have their name?

The second is confusing friends for users. It is easier to show your idea to someone who already roots for you. But a friend's yes tells you nothing. We push students toward the person who matches their persona, even though that person is harder to reach and more likely to say something that stings.

Both problems come from the same fear, which is that the test might go badly. The reframe we keep repeating is that a test going badly on Thursday is a gift. It is far cheaper to learn it now than after you have built the whole thing.

## What good looks like by Friday

By Friday you are not looking for a finished business. You are looking for a student who put something real in front of a real person and came back with a story.

The clean version of that story has a shape we practice out loud: I expected X, what actually happened was Y, and what I am changing is Z. A student who can say that has done the work of the week, even if the test was a mess. Especially if it was a mess.

The ones who tested have a number, a reaction, a surprise. The ones who haven't have one named obstacle and a plan to show someone before the week is out.

That is the posture we want them leaving with. Not certainty about the idea, but proof they can build small, put it in the world, and let a real person's reaction tell them what to do next. Test to learn, not to be right.
