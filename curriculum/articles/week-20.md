# Unique Selling Proposition + Storytelling + Brand Strategy

_Ask a student what their brand is and they point at a logo. This week we teach the harder, more useful truth: your brand is a promise, your story puts the customer at the center, and your USP is the one sharp thing nobody else can say._

Ask a room of students what a brand is and almost every hand points at the same thing. A logo. A color. A cool name. Something you draw.

That answer is not wrong, it is just small. A logo is the easiest part of a brand and the least important. You can have a beautiful logo and still be forgettable, and you can have a rough one and still be the business people trust.

So Week 20 does one job. It moves students from "my brand is what I made in Canva" to "my brand is what people feel when they think of me," and it gives them three things they can actually control to shape that feeling.

The guiding question sits over the whole week: what makes you different, and how do you tell a story that makes people care?

## Your USP is one sharp thing, not a list

We start with the Unique Selling Proposition, and the first move is to shrink it. Most students want to list ten reasons they are great. A USP is the opposite. It is the one clear reason a customer should choose you over every alternative.

We teach it as a fill-in-the-blank line so nobody can hide behind vague words:

> The only [category] that [unique benefit] for [segment].

The example we use is local and concrete: "The only meal kit in South Bend made entirely from local farm produce, delivered same-day." You can hear who it is for and why it is different in one breath.

Then we give students the test that does the real work. A good USP is true, specific, and hard to copy. If your competitor could say the exact same sentence word for word, it is not a USP yet. So I walk the room asking one question over and over: "Could someone down the street say this too?" If yes, sharpen it.

## Make the customer the hero, not yourself

The second piece is storytelling, and it comes with a reframe that surprises people every year. Students assume the story is about them - the founder, the struggle, the big idea. It is not.

People do not remember features. They remember stories, and the stories that stick make the customer the hero. The founder is not the star. The founder is the guide.

We keep the framework to four beats so a student can hold it in their head:

- **The struggle** - a customer has a problem.
- **The guide** - they find your business.
- **The solution** - you give them a plan or a product.
- **The transformation** - their life gets better.

The line that makes it land: you are not Luke Skywalker, you are Yoda. The customer is the one who wins the fight. You are the one who hands them the tool and points them at the goal.

Students write a four-sentence brand story using those beats, with the customer in the hero seat. Four sentences is on purpose. If the story needs a paragraph to work, it is not memorable yet.

## A brand is a feeling you keep consistent

The third piece is the one that breaks the "brand equals logo" habit for good. A brand is not a logo. A brand is the feeling people get when they think about you, and you shape that feeling with three things you actually control.

- **Voice** - how you sound. Playful, serious, bold, warm. Pick a lane.
- **Look** - the colors, fonts, and style that stay the same everywhere.
- **Promise** - the thing customers can always count on from you.

Then the point that ties it together: consistency is the whole game. A small brand that shows up the same way every single time feels bigger and more trustworthy than a big brand that is all over the place.

That is good news for a teenager with no budget. You cannot out-spend a real company, but you can out-consistent one, and that is often enough to earn trust.

## Where students get stuck

Two places, every time.

The first is a USP that is not actually unique. It sounds nice, but it is a claim anyone could make - "great quality, friendly service." The fix is the word-for-word test. Say it out loud, imagine a competitor saying the same line, and keep cutting until only you can say it.

The second is drift between the pieces. Their USP says one thing, their story says another, their brand voice says a third, and none of it points at the segment they picked back in Week 19. So the one-on-one question all week is simple: "Say your USP and your brand promise. Do they match the person you chose to serve?" When those three line up - segment, USP, story - the whole thing suddenly feels like a real business instead of a pile of parts.

We even pressure-test judgment on Friday with brand "deal or no deal." A designer will build you a full brand kit for $600 before you have a single customer. Deal or no deal? A big company will let you sell under their trusted name, but you lose your own brand entirely. Deal or no deal? There are no clean answers. The point is to feel the tradeoff, not to solve it fast.

## What "good" looks like by Friday

By the end of the week you are not grading logos. You are checking for alignment.

A student in good shape can do three things out loud. State their USP in the "only" format and explain what makes it hard to copy. Tell a four-sentence story where the customer, not the founder, is the hero. And name a one-sentence brand promise in a voice that matches both.

If those three point at the same segment from last week, the student is exactly where they need to be. Next week we get hands-on with the tools that put this brand in front of real people. This week we made sure there was something worth putting in front of them.
