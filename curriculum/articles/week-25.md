# Open House Prep

_A booth is not a decoration project. It is a machine for earning a stop, then a conversation, then a contact you can follow up with. Week 25 teaches students to build one._

Most students think an Open House is a craft day. Nice poster, tidy table, stand behind it, hope someone comes over. They spend an hour making it pretty and no time making it work.

That is the reframe we do this week. A booth is not decoration. It is a machine with a job: earn a stop from across the room, earn a conversation up close, and capture a contact you can follow up with later.

Once students see the booth as a machine, every design choice has a reason. The guiding question for the week is simple: how do you present your business so people understand it, remember it, and want to support it?

## Five seconds, then thirty

The whole week runs on one piece of math. You have about five seconds to earn a stop, and about thirty seconds to earn a conversation.

That math changes what you build. Five seconds means the person walking past cannot read a paragraph. They can read one bold headline and see one clear image, and that is it.

So we teach the 3-Second Rule first:

- **One bold headline** - the business name plus one line on what you do, big enough to read from across the room.
- **One strong visual** - a single product or image, not a collage.
- **A clean table** - uncluttered, because less is more and clutter reads as noise from ten feet away.

The test I have students run on each other is blunt. Stand ten feet back. What do you see, and do you get what this business does? If the answer is "I have to walk over and read it," the booth has already lost.

## The 30-second hook

If the look earns the stop, the hook earns the conversation. When someone actually pauses at your table, you need a quick, friendly opener - not your life story.

We give students a three-beat structure: problem, solution, and one interesting thing. That is the whole hook.

The example we put on the board:

> "Hi! We make fresh meal kits from local farms for busy South Bend families - dinner in 20 minutes, no grocery run. Want to see how it works?"

Notice it ends in a question, not a sales close. The goal is to open a door, not to win the sale standing there.

Then there is the part students skip: what happens after someone is interested. Do not stand behind the table on your phone. Stand beside it, make eye contact, and open with a question rather than a pitch - "Have you ever...?" beats "Let me tell you about my business" every time.

And always have a way to capture interest. A signup sheet, a QR code, a simple form. Give people a reason to leave their info - updates, a freebie, early access - and follow up within 48 hours. That last part is where the CRM from earlier in the year comes back into play.

## Nothing from scratch

Here is the thing I want facilitators to keep saying on build day: nothing this week is new work.

By Week 25 students already have the pieces. Their brand from Week 20, their core message from Week 19, their campaign visuals from Weeks 21 through 24. Tuesday is not invention. It is assembly.

So on build day students pull those pieces together into physical form:

- **A poster or sign** made in Canva or by hand, with the headline readable from across the room.
- **A one-page handout or product card** a visitor can take with them.
- **A signup sheet or QR code** ready to capture contacts.
- **A product sample or demo** if they have one.

When I walk the room on Tuesday I am asking two questions over and over. Is your headline readable from a distance? Is your table clean instead of cluttered? Most first drafts fail one of those, and the fix is usually to remove things, not add them.

## Where students get stuck

The most common failure is the wall of text. Students want to explain everything, so they fill the table with words. Nobody stops to read a wall. The fix is subtraction: one headline, one image, breathing room.

The second failure is hiding. Plenty of students will stand behind the table, avoid eye contact, and wait to be approached. So Thursday we run a booth dry run - half the class works their booths, half plays visitors, then they switch. I play a tough visitor on purpose, walking fast and looking distracted, to see who can actually earn my stop.

Visitors give real feedback against the machine's job: did I understand the business in five seconds, did the hook make me want to stay, was it easy to leave my info? Then every student writes their top two fixes before the real event.

Friday we make the lesson land with a Founder Fight. Students judge matchups - a flashy booth with a weak product against a plain booth with a strong one, a founder who talks at visitors against one who asks questions, a clear one-liner against a booth that explains everything at once. The debrief question is the whole week in one line: what actually makes people stop, stay, and remember you?

## What good looks like by Friday

By the end of the week you are not looking for the prettiest booth. You are looking for a booth that does its job.

A student is ready when they can point to a headline you can read from across the room, deliver a 30-second hook that ends in a question, and show you exactly how they will capture a contact and follow up within two days.

They should also be able to answer three things out loud in reflection: what is your one-line hook, how will you capture and follow up with people you meet, and what are you most excited or nervous to show.

If a student walks in Monday thinking this is a craft project and walks out Friday treating their table as a machine that earns a stop, a conversation, and a contact, they are exactly where they need to be. Next week the marketing is done and we make the numbers work.
