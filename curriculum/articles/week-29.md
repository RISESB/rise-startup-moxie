# Funding Options + Risk Management

_Most students think funding means finding an investor. This week we break that assumption, teach the real cost of every option, and get them naming their risks out loud before those risks bite._

Ask a student how a business gets money to grow and most of them jump straight to the same answer. Find an investor. Pitch the idea, get a check, buy the thing you need.

That answer is not wrong so much as it is one rung on a taller ladder, and for almost every high school business in the room, the wrong rung. Week 29 widens the picture, then narrows it back down to what actually fits.

The frame I open with is blunt on purpose: free money is a myth. Every way of funding a business has a cost. Some cost you speed, some cost you a relationship, some cost you a piece of the thing forever. The job this week is to see that cost clearly before you sign anything.

## The funding ladder

We start by laying out the options as rungs, from the one that keeps you most in control to the one that gives the most away.

- **Bootstrapping** - funding growth from your own sales and savings. The cost is speed. The benefit is you keep full control.
- **Friends and family** - early support from people who believe in you. The cost is that a personal relationship is now on the line.
- **Grants and competitions** - money you do not pay back, like McCloskey. The cost is the time to apply, and the use is often restricted.
- **Loans (debt)** - borrowed money you repay with interest. The cost is that you owe it no matter how the business does.
- **Investors (equity)** - money in exchange for a piece of the business. The cost is ownership and some of your control.

Reading it as a ladder does something a list does not. It shows students that investors are the top rung, not the first one, and that most of them have cheaper rungs to climb first.

## Debt versus equity, in plain terms

The two options students confuse most are debt and equity, so we slow down and separate them.

Debt means you borrow money and pay it back with interest. You keep one hundred percent ownership, but you owe the money even if the business struggles.

Equity means you sell a share of your business for money. You never repay it, but that person now owns part of everything you build, from here forward.

The line that makes it stick:

> Debt is a guest who leaves once you pay them. Equity is a roommate who never moves out. Neither one is bad. They are just different.

I want students to feel that difference, not just define it. A loan ends. A share you sold does not.

## Naming risk before it hits

The second half of the week turns to risk, and I frame it the way I actually believe it. Amateurs ignore risk and hope. Pros name it out loud and reduce it early.

We work four common risks, and each one comes with a way to lower it.

- **Customer risk** - you depend too much on one customer. Reduce it by getting more customers.
- **Supply risk** - one supplier can cut you off. Reduce it by lining up a backup.
- **Money risk** - you run out of cash. Reduce it by keeping a cushion and watching cash flow.
- **Founder risk** - everything depends on you. Reduce it by documenting how things work and building a team.

This ties straight back to effectuation from Week 9. Affordable loss is risk management wearing different clothes: never risk more than you can afford to lose. Students have met this idea before, so the callback lands deeper instead of feeling new.

## How we make it real in the room

On Tuesday students write a one-paragraph funding decision. What they need money for, if anything. How much. Which source fits. And the trade-off they accept. AI is fair game as a starting point, but the decision is theirs to own.

Then they build a risk map. Top four risks, each rated on likelihood and impact as low, medium, or high. For the highest ones, one action to reduce it. Then I have them circle their single biggest risk, because that is the one to act on first.

Friday we run Deal or No Deal, funding edition. An investor offers 10,000 dollars for thirty percent - deal or no deal, and what does that say the whole business is worth? A bank offers a loan at twelve percent - can your cash flow handle the payment in a slow month? A grant with strings - do you take money you have to spend on gear you do not need? The scenarios pull the trade-offs out of the abstract and into a real decision.

## Where students get stuck

The most common trap is wanting an investor for no real reason. It sounds like what a founder is supposed to do, so students reach for it before they have asked whether they need outside money at all.

So I walk the room and push. Many student businesses should just bootstrap. If someone wants equity, they have to defend why. Wanting to sound like a real company is not a reason to give a third of it away.

The other place they slip is treating the risk map as a worksheet to finish rather than a real look at their business. The fix is the same one all year: make it true. Their actual biggest risk, named honestly, is worth more than four safe ones.

## What good looks like by Friday

By the end of the week a student should be able to answer three questions in their own words.

What funding option fits my business right now, and why. What the difference is between debt and equity. And what my single biggest risk is, plus the one action I will take to reduce it.

The reframe to leave them with is the one we opened on. Growing usually means needing money you do not have yet, and every way of getting it has a price tag. The skilled move is not chasing a check. It is knowing which cost you are willing to pay, and naming what could go wrong before it does.
