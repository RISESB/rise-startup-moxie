# The End Part 2

_The work is done. This week is not about building anything new. It is about rehearsing the thing you built until the showcase feels like a repeat, not a leap._

Most people treat a big presentation like a performance they get one shot at. They cram the night before, hope the nerves hold, and walk in praying it goes fine.

That is exactly the wrong frame, and Week 35 exists to replace it. A showcase does not go well because a student is brave on the day. It goes well because they ran the whole thing so many times that the real event feels familiar.

So this week we build nothing new. The businesses exist. The decks exist. The job now is rehearsal, and the guiding question is blunt: can you present your business with confidence, handle real questions, and make your booth impossible to walk past?

## Rehearsal is the strategy, not the extra

Here is the reframe I want facilitators to hold all week. Confidence is not a personality trait some students have and others don't. It is a byproduct of reps.

The student who looks calm at the showcase is usually just the student who has said the words out loud the most times. Nerves shrink with familiarity, and familiarity only comes from doing the thing again and again before it counts.

So the whole week is a ramp of run-throughs. Monday is the first full delivery. Thursday is a full dress rehearsal run exactly like the real event. Friday is final fixes and a confidence close. By the time next week arrives, there should be nothing left to discover.

## The presentation skills that actually move the needle

On Monday we teach a short, practical coaching lesson before the first run-throughs. Five things, no theory:

- **Strong open** - the first 15 seconds hook them with a stat, a question, or a story. Not "um, so, my business is..."
- **Eyes up** - talk to people, not slides. Know your deck well enough that you are not reading it.
- **Slow down** - nerves speed you up, so pause, breathe, and let your points land.
- **Body** - stand tall, hands open, feet planted.
- **Strong close** - end with your ask and one memorable line, not "yeah, that's it."

Then students deliver their full presentation once, in small groups of four or five. The group gives one strength and one fix. The facilitator rotates and coaches the specific skills above.

Two things to watch for while you circle the room: reading off slides, and rushing. Those are the two habits that make a prepared student look unprepared, and they are the easiest to catch early.

## Q&A is where confidence is really tested

A polished presentation is only half of it. The other half is what happens when someone asks a question you did not script for.

So Tuesday we run a Q&A gauntlet. Students present in small groups, and the group fires real questions at them - the kind a parent, a judge, or a skeptic actually asks at a showcase:

- "How much money have you actually made?"
- "Who is your customer, exactly?"
- "What was the hardest part?"
- "Is this a real business or a school project?"
- "What is next after this year?"

The coaching line here matters more than any single answer:

> It is okay to say "I don't know yet, but here's how I'd find out." Honesty beats bluffing.

Students think they need a slick answer for everything. They don't. A calm "here's how I'd figure that out" reads as more credible than a confident guess that falls apart under a follow-up.

## The booth is half the impression

The presentation is the pitch. The booth is what makes someone stop and listen in the first place. Tuesday we plan it, reusing the Open House skills from Week 25.

A good booth has a few concrete pieces: the snapshot printed, the product or a demo, a visual or sign, a signup or contact method, and a plan for how you greet visitors instead of standing behind the table waiting.

That last one gets skipped the most. A student who plans their greeting - who says the first line to a stranger the same way they planned the first 15 seconds of the pitch - is the one whose booth is impossible to walk past.

## Where students get stuck

Rehearsal weeks have a predictable failure mode: students treat the practice as optional and save their energy for the real thing. That is backwards, and it usually shows up on Thursday.

The dress rehearsal is the tell. We run the full showcase format end to end - half the students present while half play guests, then switch, and we pull in staff or another class to walk through if we can. The students who practiced out loud at home look ready. The ones who read their deck silently on the couch discover their gaps in front of an audience, which is exactly what the rehearsal is for.

That is why Monday's homework is so specific: practice out loud three times before tomorrow, and time it. Out loud is the whole point. Reading it in your head is not rehearsal.

After the dress rehearsal, every student writes their top two fixes before the showcase. Not ten. Two. A short, honest fix list beats a long anxious one, and it gives them something concrete to close on Friday.

## What "good" looks like by Friday

By Friday you are not looking for perfection. You are looking for a student who has run the whole thing enough times that the showcase feels like one more rep.

They can deliver their pitch from memory with a real open and a real close. They can take a hard question without flinching. Their booth has a plan, and so does their greeting. They have two specific fixes made, not a vague sense of dread.

We keep Friday light on purpose - a fast, high-energy round of Deal or No Deal to shake off nerves, final fixes in 1-on-1s, and a confidence close where each student finishes the sentence "At the showcase, I want people to see ___."

That is the posture we want them walking in with next week. The work is already done. All that is left is to show it, and they have practiced showing it until it feels like theirs.
