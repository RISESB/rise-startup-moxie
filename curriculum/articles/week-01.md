# What Is Entrepreneurship? Mindset, Practice, and Life Design

_We open the year by breaking the myth. Entrepreneurship is not a personality you are born with. It is a method you can practice, starting with what you already have._

Ask a room of teenagers to picture an entrepreneur and you get the same three or four faces every time. Musk. Zuckerberg. Somebody rich, somebody famous, somebody who raised a pile of money and ended up on a magazine cover.

That picture is not wrong. It is just narrow. The founders we study in school are the lottery winners of entrepreneurship. Interesting to look at, but not the whole story, and not something a student can copy on a Tuesday afternoon.

So Week 1 does one job. It takes that myth apart and hands students a better one to build on for the rest of the year.

## A definition that lets anyone start

We put one sentence on the board and spend real time on it:

> Entrepreneurship is the pursuit of opportunity beyond the resources you currently control.

Read it slowly and it does something useful. "Pursuit of opportunity" means you see something other people are walking past. "Beyond the resources you currently control" means you do not wait until you have money, a degree, or permission. And "currently control" is the part I care about most, because it says what you have right now is enough to begin.

Nowhere in there does it say Silicon Valley. Nowhere does it say raise a round. It says notice something and start moving toward it. That reframe is the whole point of the week. A student who thinks entrepreneurship is for other people cannot start. A student who thinks it is a method they can practice can start today.

## It is a method, not a personality

Once the definition is on the table, we make it concrete with the OODA Loop. It is a decision-making cycle borrowed from fighter pilots, and it works because entrepreneurs make calls fast, usually with incomplete information.

- **Observe** - what is actually happening? What are you noticing?
- **Orient** - what does it mean, and how does it connect to what you already know?
- **Decide** - what is your move? Not the perfect move. A move.
- **Act** - do it, then loop back to Observe.

The example we use is a kid selling cookies. You keep making sugar cookies, but people keep asking for chocolate chip. You observe the pattern, orient ("this keeps happening"), decide to change the next batch, and act by baking them that night. Then you loop back and see if they sell. No market research report. No waiting. Just a loop you run over and over.

That is the quiet argument of the whole class. Entrepreneurship is not a personality type you either have or you don't. It is a way of moving through the world, and it can be practiced like any other skill.

## Traits students already have

There is a second reframe hiding in Week 1, and it matters just as much. Most students assume they are missing the traits. So on Tuesday we read real stories - Sara Blakely cutting the feet off her pantyhose and cold-calling hosiery mills, Daymond John sewing hats in his mom's house and getting LL Cool J to wear one - and we ask students to name the trait they see. Resourcefulness. Persistence. Making something out of what was already in the room.

Then we hand them a short self-assessment on six traits (curiosity, resilience, adaptability, decisiveness, vision, collaboration) and ask them to circle their top two and underline their bottom two. Not for a grade. The move is subtle but it lands: you already have some of this. The rest you can grow. Nobody starts the year believing they have nothing to work with.

## Why we start here and not with a business idea

It would be easier to open the year by making everyone pick a business. We don't, on purpose. A student who picks an idea in week one usually picks a safe one, defends it all year, and never learns to see. So Week 1 stays upstream of the idea. We ask students to fill three columns - problems they notice, things they are good at, people they want to help - and we come back to that sheet all year. That is the start of an entrepreneurial identity, and it is more durable than any single idea.

This is also where "life design" earns its place in the title. We are not only teaching students to start a business. We are teaching them to look at their own life and community as a set of problems worth solving, which is a skill that pays off whether or not they ever file an LLC.

## What "good" looks like by Friday

By the end of the week you are not looking for a polished pitch. You are looking for a shift in posture. A student who walks in on Monday thinking entrepreneurship is for famous people, and walks out Friday able to define it in their own words, name a problem that genuinely annoys them, and point to one trait they already have - that student is exactly where they need to be.

We close the week the way we will close every week: each student says one concrete thing out loud. Here it is a single sentence, "This year I want to figure out or build ______." It is small. It is supposed to be. The habit we are building is momentum, and momentum starts with saying the first thing out loud to the person next to you.

Everything else this year - the customer interviews, the pricing, the pitch, the showcase - sits on top of the reframe we do this week. Entrepreneurship is a method. You started practicing it. By May, you think differently.
