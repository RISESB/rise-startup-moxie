# Creativity + Design Thinking in Action

_Creativity gets treated like a personality trait some kids have and some don't. This week we hand students the method behind it, and put the emphasis where it belongs: solving the right problem before you build anything._

Ask most people where good ideas come from and they point at a type of person. The creative one. The one who sketches on napkins and thinks in color while the rest of us fill out spreadsheets.

That story is convenient, and it lets everyone off the hook. If creativity is something you either have or you don't, a student who doesn't feel creative gets to sit it out.

Week 8 takes that apart. Creativity is a skill, and like every skill there is a method behind it. The method is Design Thinking, and by Friday students can name it, run it, and use it on their own business idea.

## Creativity is a method, not a personality

Design Thinking came out of Stanford's d.school and it is used by teams at Apple, IDEO, and Google to build things people actually want.

The core insight is the part I want students to hold onto. Most failed products fail because the team solved the wrong problem. Design Thinking is how you make sure you are solving the right one.

We put the whole process on the board as five stages:

> Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test

- **Empathize** - understand the person who has the problem. Not what you think they need, what they actually experience. You do this with observation, conversation, and empathy maps.
- **Define** - name the core problem from the user's point of view, not yours.
- **Ideate** - generate solutions. The rule here is quantity over quality. You are generating, not judging.
- **Prototype** - pick your best idea and build the simplest version someone could react to.
- **Test** - put it in front of real users, watch what they do, then go back and improve.

The process is not a straight line. You might test something and end up all the way back at Empathize. That is not failure, that is how it works.

## Empathy is where the real work lives

If there is one stage that separates a strong idea from a weak one, it is the first one. The strongest business ideas come from the deepest empathy, and empathy is a habit, not a feeling.

So on Monday, after the lecture, we hand out empathy map templates and students fill one in for their most specific customer segment.

The map has four quadrants around the user, plus pains and gains underneath:

- **Think and Feel** - what is going on in their head about this problem?
- **Hear** - what are they hearing from friends, family, the world around them?
- **See** - what does their environment show them every day?
- **Say and Do** - what do they actually say out loud and do about it?

I am direct with students about what this is. It is a hypothesis. You are guessing based on what you know right now, and the real test is whether your guesses survive contact with an actual customer.

The facilitator's job during this block is one thing: walk the room and push for specificity in every quadrant. Vague maps produce vague problems.

## Define, then generate

Tuesday is where the process starts to move fast. Students turn the empathy map into a problem statement using a fixed format:

> [User] needs a way to [do, achieve, or feel something] because [insight].

One example we use: a first-generation college student needs a way to navigate financial aid paperwork because the language assumes knowledge she was never taught.

Students write three versions, pick the one that feels most true and most specific, and read it to the class. The rest of the room gives one word back: specific, or vague. That single word does more coaching than a paragraph would.

Then we flip into ideation, and the tone changes on purpose. Each student gets a stack of sticky notes and ten minutes on the timer. One idea per note, no editing, no judgment, ten ideas minimum.

The trick for when they stall is worth teaching directly: write the worst possible idea, then flip it. "How Might We" questions work the same way, they crack open a problem that felt closed. After ten minutes, everything goes on the wall, gets sorted, and each student picks a top three.

## Where students get stuck

The most common failure is skipping the front of the process. Some students walk in attached to a solution and want to jump straight to Prototype. Ask them who the user is and what that person actually experiences, and they go quiet.

That is the moment to name what they skipped and send them back to Empathize. It usually reshapes the idea more than any feedback you could give.

The second place they stick is confusing a prototype with a product. A prototype is a sketch, a wireframe, a storyboard, a cardboard version. The goal is to make the idea tangible enough that someone else can react to it, and that is all.

We push them past pretty. Your sketch does not have to be beautiful, it has to be specific enough that a stranger could understand what it does.

The last piece is the test plan, and it is where AI earns its keep this week. Students describe their prototype to Claude or ChatGPT and ask what questions they should ask a real user. Then they write the plan in a fixed format: I will show this to [specific person], I will ask them [these questions], I will know it is working if [this happens].

## What good looks like by Friday

You are not looking for a finished product by Friday. You are looking for a student who has run the loop.

They can name all five stages. They have an empathy map full of specifics, a problem statement someone called "specific" instead of "vague," a stack of ideas that got narrowed to one, and a rough prototype with a test plan attached.

Most of all they can answer the question we keep asking in 1-on-1s: who are you testing this with, when, and what do you expect to learn?

That is the shift. Creativity stopped being a trait some of them have and became a process all of them can run. The strongest ideas this year will come from the students who empathized deepest before they built anything, and this is the week they learn how.
