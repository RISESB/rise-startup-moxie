# Unit Economics + Revenue Projections

_Students spend twenty-five weeks learning to sell. This week they find out whether the selling makes any money. Unit economics zooms all the way in to one sale, and it changes how they read everything._

Ask a student how their business is doing and they will tell you what they sold. Ten candles. Forty stickers. A good weekend at the market. What they almost never tell you is whether they made any money, because most of them have never done the math.

That gap is the whole reason for Week 26. A student can be busy, popular, and moving product, and still lose money on every single sale. Nobody warned them that was possible.

So this week we open Unit 4: Finance by zooming all the way in. Not the whole business, not the year. One sale. If one sale does not make money, doing more of them just loses money faster.

## Revenue is not profit

We start by separating two words students use as if they were the same.

Revenue is all the money that comes in. Profit is what is left after costs. You can have ten thousand dollars in sales and zero dollars in profit if your costs were ten thousand dollars too.

The line I keep repeating is short on purpose: revenue is vanity, profit is sanity. It sticks because it is a little rude, and because most students have been quietly measuring themselves by the vanity number all semester.

The guiding question for the week sits right under this: do you actually make money on each sale, and what happens when you multiply that out?

## The core math of one sale

Once revenue and profit are separate in their heads, we hand students the one equation that runs the rest of the unit.

> Price minus variable cost equals contribution margin.

Three terms, and each one earns its place:

- **Price** - what the customer actually pays.
- **Variable cost** - what it costs you to make and deliver that one unit. Materials, packaging, fees.
- **Contribution margin** - what one sale contributes toward your fixed costs and your profit.

The example we use is a candle. It sells for twenty dollars. Wax, wick, jar, and label run seven. That leaves a contribution margin of thirteen dollars. Every candle puts thirteen dollars toward your rent, your time, and your profit.

Then we split costs into two bins, because students tend to blur them. Variable costs change with each sale, like materials. Fixed costs stay the same no matter how much you sell, like a table fee or a monthly subscription. You cover your fixed costs with the contribution margin from many sales, which is why knowing the margin on one sale matters so much.

## Turning one sale into a year

Tuesday is where the math leaves the single candle and becomes a plan.

A revenue projection is an educated guess at future revenue. Units times price, month by month. It takes a vague goal and turns it into something you can actually track.

Students build a simple twelve-month projection in a spreadsheet: units sold, price, revenue, variable costs, contribution, fixed costs, profit. Nothing fancy. The point is to see the shape of a year, not to predict it perfectly.

Then we make them build three versions. Conservative, realistic, and stretch. The question that goes with it is the useful one: which is most likely, and what would it actually take to hit the stretch?

AI helps here, but only as a second set of eyes on costs. We teach a prompt: "My product is X, I sell it for Y, here are my costs. What am I likely forgetting?" Students are almost always forgetting something. Fees, packaging, the cost of their own time.

## Where students get stuck

The honest struggle this week is not the spreadsheet. It is admitting the real cost of a sale.

Students want a clean margin, so they quietly leave things out. They count the wax but not the jar. The materials but not the payment processing fee. The product but not the hour it took to make it. When I walk the room, I am mostly asking one question over and over: did you count all of it?

The second place they get stuck is emotional. When the true numbers come out, some students discover their margin is thin, or gone. That is a hard afternoon. It is also the most valuable one in the unit, because a thin margin found in a classroom is a lesson, and a thin margin found later is a closed business.

Friday's "Deal or No Deal" round leans straight into that discomfort. A big customer wants two hundred units at your variable cost plus one dollar. A supplier will cut your per-unit cost thirty percent if you commit to a thousand units up front. You can raise your price twenty percent but might lose a third of your customers. There is no clean answer to any of them. The point is to make students argue from their own numbers instead of their gut.

## What good looks like by Friday

By the end of the week I am not looking for a business that prints money. I am looking for a student who knows their own numbers cold.

They can tell you their contribution margin on one sale, and they were probably a little surprised by it. They can explain, in plain words, the difference between their revenue and their profit. And they can point to the one number - price, cost, or volume - that would most improve their profitability, and say what they would change to move it.

We close with the reflection that matters most for the whole finance unit. Walk me through one sale. Where does the money go? Is each sale actually profitable?

A student who can answer that honestly has crossed a line. They have stopped measuring their business by how busy it feels and started measuring it by what it keeps. That is the shift the whole year has been building toward, and next week, when we get to cash flow and break-even, it is the ground everything else stands on.
