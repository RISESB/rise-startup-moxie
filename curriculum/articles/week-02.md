# Finding Your Why

_Week 1 said entrepreneurship is a method anyone can practice. Week 2 asks the harder question underneath it: a method aimed at what? This is the week students go looking for their why._

Most people think you find your why by sitting quietly and waiting for it to arrive, like a lightning bolt or a calling. That is a nice story. It almost never happens that way.

A why is usually assembled, not received. You build it out of what you love, what you are good at, what the world needs, and what someone will pay for. Then you keep sanding it down until it gets specific enough to point somewhere.

That is the job of Week 2. Last week we said entrepreneurship is a method. This week we go one layer deeper, because the best entrepreneurs do not just build businesses. They build businesses connected to who they actually are. The guiding question is plain: what drives you, and how does that connect to the kind of entrepreneur you want to become?

## Start with why, not what

The frame we open with comes from Simon Sinek. Most people and most businesses can tell you *what* they do and *how* they do it. Very few start with *why*.

We put three words on the board and spend real time on them:

> WHAT is the product or service you offer. HOW is the way you do it. WHY is the reason it matters to you.

The candle example makes it land. What: I sell handmade candles. How: natural ingredients and custom scents. Why: I believe people deserve a peaceful space at home, and I want to help create that.

Most businesses lead with the what. Great ones lead with the why. Some people build and love every minute of it, and others burn out in a year. The difference is usually whether they knew their why before they started.

## Ikigai, plus two mirrors

To move why from a nice idea to something usable, we hand every student the Ikigai diagram. Ikigai is a Japanese concept that roughly translates to "reason for being," and it sits at the intersection of four questions.

- **What do you love?**
- **What are you good at?**
- **What does the world need?**
- **What can you be paid for?**

Your sweet spot is where all four overlap. The honest part of the lesson is that most people live in only one or two circles. They love something they cannot get paid for, or they are good at something they do not love. Entrepreneurship is one path to living in all four at once.

Most teenagers cannot fill in those circles cold, though, so before the mapping we give them two mirrors.

The first is Wingfinder, a strengths assessment built by Red Bull. It sorts natural strength into four areas - Thinking, Creativity, Drive, and Influence - and it measures what comes easily to you, not what you drilled into practice. Students take it online, gut answers only, then write down their top area.

The second is 16 Personalities, based on the Myers-Briggs framework. We frame it carefully. It is not a box you live in forever. It is a mirror that shows you patterns in how you think, work, and connect, so you can work with them instead of against them. And the debrief lands a quiet lesson: notice how different everyone in the room is. A strong team is built from different types working together. That is not a problem to fix. That is the point.

## Where students get stuck: specific

Then students map their own Ikigai, using Wingfinder for what they are good at and their own self-knowledge for what they love. This is where almost everyone stalls, and it is always the same failure. They write things that are true but useless.

"Helping people" is not an answer. "Helping first-generation students navigate college applications" is. The whole skill this week is pushing past the generic to the specific, because a vague why points nowhere.

When a student freezes on the page, the fix is a smaller question: what do you do on a Saturday when nobody is telling you what to do? Start there. That answer is usually more honest than anything they would write under the word "passion."

## The About Me poster

The mapping feeds one artifact: the About Me poster, built Wednesday and Thursday. It is not a school project. It is the first version of a student's entrepreneurial brand, and it answers one question - who are you, and what do you care about?

Every poster carries the same elements:

- **Name and a word or phrase** that describes you as an entrepreneur
- **Your Ikigai sweet spot**, written out in one honest sentence
- **Your top Wingfinder strength**
- **Your 16 Personalities type**, plus one line on what it means
- **Three problems** you want to solve
- **One image or symbol** that stands for your why

The standard we hold is specificity, not art supplies. A plain poster that says "I want to help single moms in South Bend find affordable childcare" beats a beautiful one that says "I want to help people." It does not have to be perfect. It has to be honest.

Thursday each student presents for sixty to ninety seconds, and the class hands back one word they noticed on a sticky note. For many, it is the first time they have said their why out loud in front of people. That takes guts, and we say so.

## What "good" looks like by Friday

By Friday you are not looking for a finished business. You are looking for a student who can say their why in one specific sentence and connect it to a direction, even a fuzzy one.

Friday tests whether the why holds up against money. We run Deal or No Deal - a boutique wants sixty percent of every sale, an investor wants forty percent of everything forever - and after each one we ask the question that ties the week together: does this deal connect to your why, or does it just sound like money? A student who can tell the difference got the point.

The posters do not get thrown away. We pin them, keep them, and come back to them all year to watch the thinking change.

Next week we leave the classroom and go looking for problems in the community. A student who knows what drives them walks into that search seeing sharper. That is why we do this week first, and not the other way around.
