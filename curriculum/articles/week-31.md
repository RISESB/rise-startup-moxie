# Business Analytics + Data-Driven Decisions

_Students have been piling up numbers all year. This week they stop collecting and start deciding, by picking the few metrics that actually predict how the business is doing._

Most people think analytics is about having more data. The founder with the biggest spreadsheet wins.

It is close to the opposite. By this point in the year, students have plenty of numbers - sales, costs, campaign results, CRM notes. What they don't have is a way to tell which of them matter. Week 31 fixes that.

The guiding question is narrow on purpose: what are the few numbers that actually tell you how your business is doing, and how do you use them to decide? Not fifty numbers. A few.

This is also where Unit 4 begins, the stretch that carries them into operations and the showcase. So the week does double duty: it teaches a skill and turns a year of scattered data into something they can read.

## Analytics is turning numbers into decisions

We open Monday with a plain definition and sit on it for a minute.

> Analytics is turning your numbers into decisions. The best founders are not the ones with the most data. They are the ones who watch the right few numbers and act on them.

That reframe does the heavy lifting. A student who thinks analytics means "track everything" freezes. A student who thinks it means "watch three numbers and respond" can start this morning.

From there we name the tool: the KPI, or Key Performance Indicator. A number that predicts the health of the business. Key is the word that matters. You could track fifty things. You should track three to five.

We put the common small-business KPIs on the board as a menu:

- **Revenue** - money in over a period.
- **Units sold or customers served** - how much you actually moved.
- **Conversion rate** - how many interested people actually buy.
- **Customer acquisition cost** - what it costs to get one customer.
- **Repeat rate** - how many come back.

Then the rule that keeps it honest: pick the ones that match your goal. If your goal is growth, watch new customers. If your goal is profit, watch margin and repeat rate. The KPI follows the goal, not the other way around.

## Vanity numbers flatter you. Real numbers move you.

The trap here is the number that feels good and does nothing. We call it a vanity metric and name it out loud so students can spot their own.

The line sticks: a thousand followers and zero sales is a vanity number. Forty followers and twelve buyers is a real one. Watch what matters, not what flatters.

This lands harder than you would expect, because most students have a number they are secretly proud of. A follower count. A view count. Part of the Monday work block is crossing those out, on purpose, and it stings a little. That sting is the lesson.

A real metric connects to money or customers. If a number cannot change a decision, it is not key.

## One number means nothing. The trend means everything.

The second half of the concept is reading across time. A single number is a dead end. A hundred dollars in sales this week means nothing until you know last week was sixty, which is growth, or two hundred, which is a problem.

So we teach them to always compare across time. Up, down, or flat is where the story lives.

And this connects straight back to Week 1. The data loop is the OODA Loop wearing a spreadsheet: track, notice a trend, ask why, make one change, track again. Same cycle they have run all year, now powered by their actual numbers instead of a gut feeling.

## How we actually teach it: build the dashboard, then read it

The concept is Monday. The building is Tuesday, and it is deliberately low-tech. Students build a simple dashboard in a spreadsheet: KPIs down one side, weeks across the top, a spot to log each number over time. Then they fill in whatever real data they have from the year. Even a few weeks of history shows a trend, and a trend is the point.

We hand them an AI prompt to skip the fiddly part: *I want to track [KPIs] in Google Sheets. How should I set up a simple dashboard, and what formula gives me week-over-week change?* AI does the setup. The student does the reading.

Because reading is the real skill. After the build, every student writes one sentence: my clearest trend is ___, it is going up, down, or flat, and my best guess for why is ___. That sentence is worth more than the spreadsheet.

By Thursday it turns into a decision. On the Data Work Day they finish the dashboard in their portfolio and commit to one move: my data says ___, so I am going to ___. In 1-on-1s I ask one thing - what is your data telling you, and what will you do about it?

## Where students get stuck

Two places, every time.

The first is over-collecting. They want ten KPIs because cutting feels like losing. Walk the room and keep asking: does this number connect to money or customers? If not, is it really key? Force it down to three to five.

The second is reading a single number as if it settled something. A student sees this week's sales and declares victory or disaster. Pull them back to the trend. One data point is a dot. You need two to draw a line, and the line is the answer.

## What good looks like by Friday

By Friday you are not looking for a polished analytics report. You are looking for a founder who can point at a dashboard and tell you the story in it.

The strongest version has three things: three to five KPIs they can defend, one clear trend they can name, and one real decision they made because of it. Friday's Founder Fight sharpens the point - gut versus data, five thousand followers versus fifty paying customers - and the debrief refuses the easy answer. When is instinct useful? When does data have to win?

The reframe to leave them with is small and durable. You do not have to guess how the business is doing. Watch a few honest numbers, read the trend, and let it tell you what to do next.
